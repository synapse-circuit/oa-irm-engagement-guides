On-Call Schedules

A practical guide to designing, operating, and scaling on-call schedules in Grafana Cloud IRM. Written for platform teams configuring schedules and for engineers who will be on-call.

1. Why on-call schedules matter#1-why-on-call-schedules-matter

On-call schedules determine who receives pages when escalation chains execute. Well-designed schedules ensure reliable coverage while respecting team members' time. In Grafana IRM, alerts flow through this path:

text
Alert → Route → Escalation Chain → Schedule → On-call Responder

Escalation chains reference schedules through notification steps ("Notify users from on-call schedule"), so schedule design and escalation design go hand in hand.

Key concept — dynamic evaluation

IRM evaluates schedules at *execution time*, not when the alert was created. If someone swaps a shift mid-escalation, the new on-call person receives the next notification. This differs from escalation chains, which are snapshotted when an alert group is created.

2. Core concepts#2-core-concepts

ConceptDefinition
ScheduleThe container that defines who is on-call at any given time; holds one or more shifts/rotations
RotationA recurring pattern (daily, weekly, custom) that generates on-call shifts
ShiftA specific time period when a person or group is responsible for responding
LayerConcurrent rotation patterns; shifts in higher layers override lower ones
OverrideA one-time exception for time off or special coverage; doesn't change the underlying rotation
Shift swapAn exchange of shifts between two users; maintains rotation continuity and is easier to audit
Recurrence groupA group of users on-call simultaneously within a rotation; all members are notified together

3. Choose the right schedule type#3-choose-the-right-schedule-type

Grafana IRM supports three management approaches:

TypeManaged viaBest forNotes
WebIRM UITeams new to on-call, simple rotations, visual editingDrag-and-drop editing, rotation preview, override management
iCalExternal calendar (e.g. Google Calendar)Migrations, teams with existing calendar-based schedulesChanges require editing the external calendar; IRM syncs periodically; limited in-app editing
API / TerraformCodeInfrastructure-as-code teams, automation, many teams at scaleFull API control; can enable web-based overrides on top of code-managed rotations
Rule of thumb

Web for simplicity, iCal for integration, API/Terraform for automation.

4. Rotation design best practices#4-rotation-design-best-practices

Pick a rotation length that matches your alert volume

PatternDurationUse case
Daily24 hoursHigh-frequency alerts, distributed teams
Weekly7 daysMost common; good work-life balance
Bi-weekly14 daysSmaller teams, less frequent alerts

Combine rotation frequency with shift length to fit your team: 12-hour shifts with a weekly rotation (two rotations covering day and night), business-hours shifts (9:00–18:00), or extended 36-hour shifts on a bi-daily rotation.

Set the rotation start explicitly

Don't rely on default behavior. Distinguish shift start (when the shift pattern begins each day/week) from rotation start (when the rotation through users begins) — set the rotation start deliberately when you want handoffs aligned to a specific date, such as a sprint boundary.

Use recurrence groups for shared responsibility

Group multiple users in one recurrence group to put them on-call simultaneously. Common patterns: co-primary responders, follow-the-sun (groups per time zone), graduated response (junior/senior pairing), shadow coverage for onboarding, and backup coverage during critical periods.

Layer schedules for escalation depth

Use separate schedules for primary, secondary, and management tiers, referenced from one escalation chain:

text
1. Notify on-call from "Primary Schedule"
2. Wait 5 minutes
3. Notify on-call from "Secondary Schedule"
4. Wait 10 minutes
5. Notify on-call from "Management Schedule"

Primary is the first line of defense (people most familiar with the system), secondary backs up if primary doesn't acknowledge in time, and tertiary is the final escalation point.

Common rotation patterns

Documented examples you can adapt (see schedule examples):

  1. Weekly rotation with 12-hour daily shifts (day/night split)
  2. Bi-daily rotation during workdays (weekday mask on)
  3. Bi-daily rotation with 36-hour shifts (weekday mask off)
  4. Weekly rotation with 9-hour workday shifts (9:00–18:00)
  5. Bi-weekly rotation with 8-hour daily shifts (repeat groups in the list to alternate every two weeks)
  6. Team rotation with multiple users per shift (e.g. Group 1: Alice + Bob → Group 2: Charlie → Group 3: Diana + Eve)

5. Time zones and distributed teams#5-time-zones-and-distributed-teams

  • Enable timezone support for new web schedules: shifts respect the schedule's timezone and daylight saving time is handled automatically. Legacy UTC-stored shifts require manual DST adjustment and cause cross-timezone confusion.
  • Test timezone changes carefully. A rotation starting "Monday 9am" in US/Pacific may fall on Monday or Tuesday in UTC depending on DST; changing a schedule's timezone can unexpectedly shift the rotation day.
  • For follow-the-sun coverage, create one rotation (or recurrence group) per region and verify handoff times land inside each region's working hours.

6. Handling changes: swaps, overrides, and priorities#6-handling-changes-swaps-overrides-and-priorities

  • Prefer shift swaps for planned changes. The swapped user returns to their normal position afterward, rotation continuity is maintained, and swaps are easier to track and audit.
  • Use overrides for last-minute coverage. Overrides create a one-time exception without altering the underlying rotation.
  • Set priorities intentionally. When shifts overlap, higher priority wins. Overrides typically use priority 99 (highest); primary shifts use lower priorities (0–10).
  • When editing rotations, choose Save as new (rather than Update) to preserve rotation history and version your rotations.

7. Monitor schedule quality#7-monitor-schedule-quality

Grafana IRM scores each schedule from 0–100:

ScoreRatingMeaning
81–100GreatWell-balanced, consistent coverage
61–80GoodMinor improvements possible
41–60MediumSome gaps or imbalances
21–40LowSignificant improvements needed
0–20PoorMajor coverage issues

Practices to keep quality high:

  • Enable gap and empty-shift reports. Gaps (periods with no coverage) and empty shifts (shifts with no assigned users) both cause missed alerts.
  • Review balance scores regularly to catch overloaded members; adjust rotations if some users consistently carry more load.
  • Account for holidays and time off when assessing fairness, and consider timezone distribution for follow-the-sun teams.
  • Note that deleted users or users lacking on-call permissions also lower the score.
  • Enable Slack notifications for weekly gap reports and shifts missing users.

8. Onboarding responders#8-onboarding-responders

  • Users need Admin or Editor roles to be assigned shifts; Viewer-role users cannot be on-call or receive alert notifications.
  • Every responder must configure personal notification rules (default and important chains) — a schedule is only as good as the notifications behind it.
  • Have responders install the Grafana IRM mobile app and connect Slack in their IRM profile (required for auto-updated Slack on-call user groups).
  • When testing a new schedule, add yourself as the current on-call user first to avoid paging teammates.
  • Verify with the Final schedule view and the On-call now indicator before wiring the schedule to production escalation chains.

9. Schedules as code (Terraform and API)#9-schedules-as-code-terraform-and-api

Managing schedules as code brings automation and efficiency, consistency across teams, version control and collaboration through Git, CI/CD integration, and confident scaling — replicate proven rotation patterns across dozens of teams.

Terraform

The Grafana Terraform provider manages schedules with these resources:

  • grafana_oncall_schedule — the schedule container (type = "calendar" for Terraform-defined shifts; enable_web_overrides = true lets responders manage overrides in the UI while code owns the rotations)
  • grafana_oncall_on_call_shift — the rotation pattern (type = "rolling_users" is the standard choice; recurrent_event is deprecated)
  • grafana_oncall_escalation — connects the schedule to an escalation chain
  • Data sources: grafana_oncall_user, grafana_oncall_team, grafana_oncall_slack_channel, grafana_oncall_user_group

Provider setup:

terraform
provider "grafana" {
  alias      = "oncall"
  url        = "<Stack-URL>"
  auth       = "<Service-account-token>"
  oncall_url = "<OnCall-URL>"
}

A complete weekly rotation:

terraform
data "grafana_oncall_user" "user_one" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  username = "user_one"
}

data "grafana_oncall_user" "user_two" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  username = "user_two"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_on_call_shift" "week_shift" {
  provider   = grafana.oncall
  name       = "Week shift"
  type       = "rolling_users"
  start      = "2025-03-01T00:00:00"
  duration   = 60 * 60 * 24  # 24 hours in seconds
  frequency  = "weekly"
  by_day     = ["MO", "TU", "WE", "TH", "FR", "SA", "SU"]
  week_start = "MO"
  rolling_users = [
    [data.grafana_oncall_user.user_one.id],
    [data.grafana_oncall_user.user_two.id]
  ]
  time_zone = "UTC"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_schedule" "primary" {
  provider  = grafana.oncall
  name      = "Primary"
  type      = "calendar"
  time_zone = "UTC"
  shifts    = [grafana_oncall_on_call_shift.week_shift.id]
}

resource "grafana_oncall_escalation" "notify_schedule" {
  provider                     = grafana.oncall
  escalation_chain_id          = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  type                         = "notify_on_call_from_schedule"
  notify_on_call_from_schedule = grafana_oncall_schedule.primary.id
  position                     = 0
}

Scaling tip: build rolling_users dynamically from a team map to stamp out consistent regional rotations:

terraform
resource "grafana_oncall_on_call_shift" "emea_weekday_shift" {
  name       = "EMEA Weekday Shift"
  type       = "rolling_users"
  start      = "2022-02-28T03:00:00"
  duration   = 60 * 60 * 12 # 12 hours
  frequency  = "weekly"
  interval   = 1
  by_day     = ["MO", "TU", "WE", "TH", "FR"]
  week_start = "MO"
  rolling_users = [for k in flatten([
    local.teams_map_of_user_id.emea,
  ]) : [k]]
  start_rotation_from_user_index = 0
  team_id = data.grafana_oncall_team.my_team.id
}

Key details to remember: duration is in seconds; start uses yyyy-MM-dd'T'HH:mm:ss; level sets shift priority; start_rotation_from_user_index controls who starts first; existing schedules can be imported with terraform import grafana_oncall_schedule.name "{{ id }}".

API

For custom integrations or dynamic management, use the IRM Schedules API:

bash
curl -X POST "https://<OnCall-API-URL>/api/v1/schedules/" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $GRAFANA_API_KEY" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "name": "Primary On-Call Schedule",
    "team_id": "your-team-id",
    "type": "calendar",
    "time_zone": "UTC"
  }'

Endpoints: GET/POST /api/v1/schedules/, PUT/DELETE /api/v1/schedules/{schedule_id}.

As-code best practices

  • Use source control — store Terraform configurations and API scripts in Git to track changes
  • Automate deployments — use CI/CD (GitHub Actions, Terraform Cloud) to manage schedules across environments
  • Apply RBAC — restrict who can modify schedules via API tokens and IAM policies
  • Test before applying — always run terraform plan before deploying to production
  • Use variables and modules — create reusable schedule patterns and stamp them out per team
  • Document your approach — maintain documentation of schedule structure and rotation patterns
  • Implement monitoring — alert on schedule configuration changes or failures
  • Always specify time zones per rotation and test across DST boundaries for long-lived rotations

10. Quick reference checklist#10-quick-reference-checklist

  • Choose schedule type: web (simplicity), iCal (integration), API/Terraform (automation)
  • Match rotation length to alert volume (weekly is the most common default)
  • Set rotation start explicitly; align handoffs to working hours
  • Enable timezone support; verify DST behavior
  • Use recurrence groups for co-primary/shadow patterns
  • Build primary → secondary → management escalation tiers
  • Set override priority high (99); primary shifts low (0–10)
  • Prefer shift swaps for planned changes; overrides for last-minute cover
  • Enable quality reports; keep score above 80
  • Ensure all responders have Editor+ roles and notification rules configured
  • Manage at scale with Terraform modules + Git + CI/CD

Escalation Chains

A practical guide to designing, operating, and scaling escalation chains in Grafana Cloud IRM. Written for platform teams configuring escalation and for engineers who respond to alerts. Companion to the On-Call Schedules Best Practices Guide.

1. What escalation chains do#1-what-escalation-chains-do

An escalation chain is a sequence of steps that IRM executes when an alert group is created. Each step can notify users, wait for a response, or perform actions like declaring an incident. Chains automate your incident response workflow by executing ordered steps until an alert is acknowledged, resolved, or all steps complete.

Escalation chains sit at the center of the alert flow:

text
Alert → Route → Escalation Chain → Schedule → Responder

Alerts arrive via integrations (Grafana Alerting, Prometheus, webhooks, and so on). Routes — configured per integration — determine which escalation chain handles an alert group. The chain then executes its steps in order, referencing schedules (who is on-call now) and each responder's personal notification rules (how that person is contacted).

Key concept — snapshots

IRM snapshots the escalation chain when it creates an alert group. Chain configuration, steps, and user queue positions are frozen at that moment; changes to a chain don't affect alert groups already using it. Schedules are the opposite: the chain stores only a *reference* to the schedule, and IRM checks who is currently on-call when the step actually executes. Two practical consequences: always test chain changes with *new* alerts, and rest easy that shift swaps take effect immediately even mid-escalation.

2. Escalation step types#2-escalation-step-types

Notification steps

StepWhat it does
Notify usersSend notifications to specific users
Notify from on-call scheduleAlert the currently on-call user(s) of a schedule
Notify next on-call shift from scheduleNotify all users of the *next* shift (14-day lookahead; skipped if none)
Notify all team membersAlert everyone in a specified team
Notify Slack channel / user groupNotify via Slack (whole-channel pings all members — use with care)
Notify users one by one (round robin)Cycle through users across *different* alert groups, sorted by username

Round robin distributes load *across* alert groups (group 1 → user A, group 2 → user B). To notify multiple users within the same alert group until someone acknowledges, use multiple Notify steps separated by Wait steps.

Default vs Important

every notification step targets one of the two personal notification rule sets in each user's IRM profile. Default is for standard notifications; Important is for high-priority notifications that use more aggressive channels or frequencies. Responders must configure both rule sets for Important steps to behave differently.

Timing and control steps

StepWhat it does
WaitPause for a set duration (60 seconds – 24 hours)
Repeat escalationLoop the previous steps up to 5 times per repeat step
Continue if current UTC time is in rangeTime-based gating (e.g. 09:00:00Z–18:00:00Z)
Notify if number of alerts in time windowVolume threshold gating (more than X alerts in Y minutes)

To repeat more than five times, add multiple repeat steps — each has its own counter; when exhausted, escalation advances to the next step.

Action steps

StepWhat it does
Resolve incident automaticallyMarks the alert group "Resolved automatically"
Trigger outgoing webhookSend data to an external system (ticketing, remediation)
Declare incidentCreates a Grafana Incident with a specified severity

Declare incident works only on non-default routes, and is limited to one active incident per route — additional alerts are grouped into the active incident.

3. The basic chain pattern#3-the-basic-chain-pattern

Most chains should follow this shape:

text
1. Notify on-call from schedule
2. Wait 5 minutes
3. Notify on-call from schedule (important)
4. Wait 10 minutes
5. Notify backup schedule

Start standard, wait for acknowledgment, escalate to important, and eventually reach a backup. Start simple — notify, wait, escalate — and add sophistication only when metrics show you need it.

Choose wait durations by priority

WaitUse case
1 minuteUrgent alerts, quick acknowledgment expected
5 minutesStandard alerts, reasonable response time
15 minutesLower priority, allows investigation time
30–60 minutesInformational alerts, batch processing

Start with longer waits and shorten based on actual response times. Wait steps space out notifications and prevent notification fatigue.

End every chain definitively

Without a terminal step, escalation can simply stop with nobody responding. Close every chain with one of: Resolve (auto-resolve if no response is needed), Notify all (escalate to an entire team or channel as a last resort), or Repeat (restart the chain a limited number of times — the 5-repeat cap prevents infinite loops).

Use Important sparingly

Reserve Important notifications for genuine escalation within a chain — after initial notification attempts fail, or for truly critical alerts. Overusing them reduces their effectiveness. The standard pattern: notify (default) → wait 5 minutes → notify (important).

4. Proven example chains#4-proven-example-chains

Critical system

— fast, loud, ends in an incident:

text
1. Notify primary on-call (important)
2. Notify Slack channel #critical-alerts
3. Wait 5 minutes
4. Notify backup on-call (important)
5. Wait 2 minutes
6. Notify all team members (important)
7. Wait 2 minutes
8. Declare incident (severity: critical)

Shadow/onboarding coverage:

text
1. Notify from Primary Schedule
2. Wait 5 minutes
3. Notify from Shadow Schedule
4. Wait 5 minutes
5. Notify next on-call shift from Primary Schedule
Business hours vs after hours

— two schedules, one chain. This works because when an escalation step attempts to notify from a schedule that has no one on-call, that step is automatically skipped:

text
1. Notify from "Business Hours Team" (important)
2. Wait 5 minutes
3. Notify from "After Hours Individual" (important)

Alternative: a single 24/7 schedule with different shifts, which is simpler to manage and scales better.

Volume throttling

— avoid paging for noise while still escalating real problems: gate the chain with "Notify if number of alerts in time window" (e.g. more than 5 alerts in 30 minutes → continue; otherwise stop).

5. Routes: getting alerts to the right chain#5-routes-getting-alerts-to-the-right-chain

Routes are configured per integration and evaluated top to bottom — first match wins.

  • Write routing conditions as Jinja2 templates that evaluate to True, e.g. {{ payload.severity == "critical" }} or label-based: {{ labels.team == "database" }}.
  • Route by team, severity, or environment — and reuse generic severity-based chains across multiple teams' routes.
  • Always keep a catch-all default route ({{ True }}) at the bottom so no alert falls through.
  • Use the template preview with sample payloads to test conditions; {{ payload }} helps debug what's actually arriving.
  • Routes also control ChatOps delivery: the route defines which Slack channel receives the alert group; the notify_whole_channel escalation step controls when that channel is pinged. You can't post to one channel and notify a different one.

6. Organizing chains at scale#6-organizing-chains-at-scale

  • One chain per escalation path. Separate fast and slow paths: payments-critical (rapid escalation) vs payments-warning (slower), platform-business-hours vs platform-24x7.
  • Name clearly. Include team/service, severity, and time behavior: auth-team-critical-24x7, data-pipeline-business-hours, infrastructure-p1-immediate.
  • Reuse chains across routes. A chain can serve multiple routes — build generic severity-based chains that many teams use, plus team-specific chains shared across that team's services.
  • Document your chains. Maintain a short explanation of each chain's purpose and expected behavior.
  • Check before deleting. Review the "Linked integrations and routes" panel first — changes affect every associated integration and route.

7. Testing and tuning#7-testing-and-tuning

Before deploying a chain: create a test integration, send test alerts through it, and verify notifications reach the right people at the right times. Because of snapshotting, always test with new alerts — editing a chain does nothing for alert groups already in flight.

Monitor three metrics and tune accordingly:

  • Time to first acknowledgment — slow? Add Important-notification steps earlier.
  • Escalation depth (how many steps run before response) — consistently deep? Shorten waits or add notification channels.
  • False escalations — frequent? Improve alert quality or add volume throttling.

8. Onboarding responders#8-onboarding-responders

  • Chains decide *who and when*; each user's personal notification rules decide *how*. Every responder must configure both the Default and Important rule sets — otherwise Important steps escalate nothing.
  • Acknowledging an alert group stops escalation; make sure responders know to acknowledge promptly, even before investigating.
  • When unsure whether a step should be Default or Important, start with Default and escalate to Important later in the chain.

9. Escalation chains as code (Terraform and API)#9-escalation-chains-as-code-terraform-and-api

Managing chains as code brings the same benefits as schedules: version control, review workflows, reuse of proven escalation patterns, and consistency across dozens of teams.

Terraform

Two resources model chains: grafana_oncall_escalation_chain (the container) and grafana_oncall_escalation (one resource per step, ordered by position starting from 0).

terraform
data "grafana_team" "my_team" { name = "my team" }
data "grafana_oncall_team" "my_team" { name = data.grafana_team.my_team.name }

resource "grafana_oncall_escalation_chain" "default" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  name     = "default"
  team_id  = data.grafana_oncall_team.my_team.id
}

A complete basic pattern as code:

terraform
# Step 0: notify current on-call
resource "grafana_oncall_escalation" "notify_oncall" {
  escalation_chain_id          = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  type                         = "notify_on_call_from_schedule"
  notify_on_call_from_schedule = grafana_oncall_schedule.primary.id
  position                     = 0
}

# Step 1: wait 5 minutes
resource "grafana_oncall_escalation" "wait_5m" {
  escalation_chain_id = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  type                = "wait"
  duration            = 300
  position            = 1
}

# Step 2: notify again using important notification rules
resource "grafana_oncall_escalation" "notify_important" {
  escalation_chain_id          = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  type                         = "notify_on_call_from_schedule"
  notify_on_call_from_schedule = grafana_oncall_schedule.primary.id
  important                    = true
  position                     = 2
}

# Step 3: notify the next upcoming shift as backup
resource "grafana_oncall_escalation" "notify_next_shift" {
  escalation_chain_id          = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  type                         = "notify_next_on_call_from_schedule"
  notify_on_call_from_schedule = grafana_oncall_schedule.primary.id
  position                     = 3
}

Step type values: wait, notify_persons, notify_person_next_each_time (round robin), notify_on_call_from_schedule, notify_next_on_call_from_schedule, trigger_webhook, notify_user_group, resolve, notify_whole_channel, notify_if_time_from_to, notify_if_num_alerts_in_window, repeat_escalation, notify_team_members, declare_incident.

Key arguments: duration (wait, 60–86400 seconds); important (bool, valid on person/schedule/group/team notify steps); persons_to_notify / persons_to_notify_next_each_time (user ID sets); notify_on_call_from_schedule (schedule ID, used by both schedule step types); group_to_notify; action_to_trigger (webhook ID); notify_if_time_from / notify_if_time_to (UTC, e.g. 08:00:00Z/18:00:00Z); num_alerts_in_window / num_minutes_in_window; severity (for declare_incident). Import existing resources with terraform import grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.name "{{ id }}".

Slack note: to notify a whole channel, set slack_channel_id on the grafana_oncall_route and use a notify_whole_channel step — the route defines where the alert group is delivered; the escalation controls when the channel is pinged.

API

Authenticate with Authorization: Bearer <token> plus X-Grafana-URL: https://your-stack.grafana.net. Required permissions: grafana-irm-app.escalation-chains:read / :write.

Chains: GET/POST {{API_URL}}/api/v1/escalation_chains/, GET/DELETE .../<ID>/. Steps ("escalation policies"): GET/POST {{API_URL}}/api/v1/escalation_policies/ (same 14 types; position: -1 appends to the end), GET/PUT/DELETE .../<ID>/.

Useful operational endpoints on a chain: GET /<ID>/current_oncall/ resolves everyone who would be notified before the first Wait step (with phone status: available / private / not_configured) — handy for pre-shift coverage checks; /previous_oncall/ and /next_oncall/ accept a days parameter (default 7, max 30); POST /escalation_policies/<ID>/advance_round_robin/ advances a round-robin pointer.

As-code best practices

  • Model each step as its own resource with explicit position values — reordering is a one-line diff in review.
  • Wrap standard patterns in modules: a "critical-24x7" module taking a schedule ID and Slack channel stamps out identical escalation behavior for every team.
  • Keep chains, schedules, and routes in the same Terraform workspace so a single terraform plan shows the full blast radius of a change.
  • Use source control + CI/CD: PR review for every escalation change, terraform plan in CI, apply on merge.
  • RBAC on tokens: service-account tokens scoped with least privilege; restrict who can modify escalation config.
  • Remember snapshots when rolling out: applied changes only affect *new* alert groups — schedule a test alert after each apply to validate the new chain.

10. Quick reference checklist#10-quick-reference-checklist

  • Every integration has routes ending in a {{ True }} catch-all
  • One chain per escalation path, named team-severity-timebehavior
  • Chains follow: notify → wait → notify (important) → backup
  • Wait durations match alert priority (1 / 5 / 15 / 30–60 min)
  • Every chain ends definitively (resolve, notify-all, or capped repeat)
  • Important reserved for genuine escalation

  • Time to acknowledgment, escalation depth, and false escalations reviewed regularly
  • Chains managed as code: one resource per step, modules for standard patterns

Integrations

A practical guide to connecting alert sources to Grafana Cloud IRM and shaping alerts with templates, grouping, and routing. Written for platform teams configuring integrations and for engineers who consume the resulting alerts. Companion to the On-Call Schedules and Escalation Chains guides.

1. What integrations are#1-what-integrations-are

Integrations are the entry points for alerts into Grafana IRM. Each integration is a unique HTTP endpoint that receives payloads from a monitoring system, plus the configuration that shapes those payloads into actionable alert groups.

Every payload follows a fixed lifecycle:

text
Payload arrives → Templates format it → Routes select a chain → Grouping ID evaluated → Alert group → Escalation chain runs

Four principles govern this pipeline:

  1. Routing happens before grouping.
  2. Each integration has its own routing and grouping configuration — settings never apply across integrations.
  3. Alerts on different routes never group together, even with identical grouping IDs.
  4. Each payload is an independent event — IRM never updates past payloads.

Think of routing as two stages: *source routing* (Grafana Alerting notification policies or Alertmanager routes deliver alerts to the right team's integration — coarse-grained) and *IRM routing* (routes inside the integration select escalation chains by severity or service — fine-grained).

2. Available alert sources and how they connect#2-available-alert-sources-and-how-they-connect

Built-in alert source integrations include Grafana Alerting, Alertmanager/Prometheus (also Mimir/Cortex), Amazon SNS (the path for CloudWatch), Datadog, Zabbix, New Relic, Sentry, Pingdom, PRTG, ElastAlert, Kapacitor, AppDynamics, UptimeRobot, Stackdriver (Google Cloud Monitoring), Inbound Email, Jira, ServiceNow, Zendesk — plus generic Webhook and Formatted Webhook integrations for anything else.

Nearly all connect the same way: IRM generates a unique endpoint URL with an embedded token, and you paste it into the source tool's webhook or notification configuration. Source-specific notes:

  • Grafana Alerting (same stack): use "Quick connect" on the integration tile — it creates the integration and a matching contact point; you still need to attach that contact point to a notification policy or alert rule. For an external Grafana instance, use the Alertmanager integration type instead.
  • Alertmanager/Prometheus: add a webhook_configs receiver with the integration URL (trailing slash required), send_resolved: true (enables auto-resolution), and max_alerts: 100 or lower to avoid oversized requests.
  • CloudWatch: alarm → SNS topic → HTTPS subscription pointing at the IRM Amazon SNS integration URL.
  • Inbound Email: IRM provisions a dedicated email address; alerts group by subject and auto-resolve when the body equals "OK".
  • Formatted Webhook: parses recognized fields — alert_uid (grouping), title, state (ok/alerting, drives auto-resolution), message, image_url, link_to_upstream_details. None are required, but including them improves handling. The plain Webhook type ingests any raw JSON as-is.

3. Integration topology: one per team#3-integration-topology-one-per-team

The single highest-leverage design decision: create one integration per team, and configure source-side label selectors so each team's integration receives only its own alerts. This keeps routing manageable, makes ownership unambiguous, and lets each team tune templates and grouping independently.

Also:

  • Assign every integration to a team for ownership and visibility.
  • Use purpose-revealing names ("Payments Alertmanager", "MyService heartbeat").
  • Maintain records of webhook sources and configurations — an integration inventory pays for itself during incidents.

4. Secure the endpoints#4-secure-the-endpoints

By default, any request to an integration URL is accepted — the URL's embedded token is the only protection. For sensitive environments, enable "Require a Grafana service account token to send alerts to this integration" in the integration settings. Requests must then carry Authorization: Bearer glsa_... from a service account with the grafana-irm-app.alert-groups:write permission; anything else gets 403 Forbidden.

Notes: bearer-token protection isn't available for Inbound Email or Amazon SNS (they can't attach headers). For Grafana Alerting contact points, add the token in the Authentication Header fields.

5. Alert templates (Jinja2)#5-alert-templates-jinja2

Templates transform raw JSON payloads into readable, actionable alerts. Three families:

  • Routing templates — must evaluate to True for a route to match, e.g. {{ "database" in payload.title | lower }}.
  • Appearance templates — per channel (Web, Slack, MS Teams, Telegram, SMS, phone call, email, mobile app): title, message, image URL.
  • Behavioral templates — grouping ID, auto-resolution signal, auto-acknowledge, source link.

Templates reference payload (raw alert data) and labels. Every integration ships with sensible defaults per source — customize only where it adds value.

Template design practices:

  • Add context responders act on: dashboard links, runbook URLs, service and instance labels, current values.
  • Use conditional formatting by severity so critical alerts look critical.
  • Keep SMS and phone-call titles concise — they're read aloud or truncated.
  • Test with real data: the template editor shows example payload, editor, and rendered result side by side; load a recent alert group or paste a custom payload.

Example message template (from the docs):

jinja2
{% set severity = payload.labels.severity | default('unknown') %}

**{{ payload.alertname }}**
{% if severity == 'critical' %}🔴 CRITICAL{% elif severity == 'warning' %}🟠 WARNING{% else %}ℹ️ INFO{% endif %}

**Details:**
- Service: {{ payload.labels.service | default('N/A') }}
- Instance: {{ payload.labels.instance | default('N/A') }}
- Value: {{ payload.value | default('N/A') }} {{ payload.unit | default('') }}

**Runbook:** {{ payload.annotations.runbook_url | default('No runbook available') }}

**Dashboard:** {{ payload.generatorURL | default('N/A') }}

6. Alert grouping: the noise killer#6-alert-grouping-the-noise-killer

The difference between good and poor grouping can mean receiving 1 phone call instead of 10 at 4am.

Two payloads join the same alert group when they render the same Grouping ID, arrive on the same route, and an *open* group (Firing, Acknowledged, or Silenced) with that ID exists. Resolved groups are closed — a matching new payload starts a new group.

  • Alertmanager and Grafana Alerting: keep the default {{ payload.groupKey }} template, and configure grouping at the source (notification policies — commonly alertname + grafana_folder, or alertname + namespace + cluster). Changing it breaks auto-resolution.
  • Other sources: compose a stable key, e.g. {{ payload.alertname }}:{{ payload.labels.service }}, or reuse native IDs (Datadog monitor ID, incident keys).
  • Good identifiers: alertname, service_name, namespace, groupKey. Never group on status fields (Firing/Resolved/OK) — fire and resolve events land in different groups and auto-resolution breaks. Avoid timestamps and unique per-event IDs (every alert becomes its own group).
  • Enable auto-resolution (allow_source_based_resolving) for sources that send resolve signals; pair with send_resolved: true on the source.
  • Notification templates render against a single payload of the group, so add differentiating context to the message template if grouped alerts look identical.
  • Prefer time-limited silences; review any "Silence forever" groups periodically — without auto-resolution they absorb alerts indefinitely.
  • Consider requiring resolution notes (is_resolution_note_required) to capture knowledge at close-out.

7. Routes inside the integration#7-routes-inside-the-integration

  • Order routes most-specific first; evaluation is top-to-bottom, first match wins.
  • Prefer Jinja2 label/payload conditions over regex — regex route matching is deprecated and unavailable in new integrations.
  • Always end with a catch-all default route attached to a real escalation chain. Don't treat the default route as a dumping ground for alerts you want to ignore — some teams deliberately make it their *critical* path so unmatched alerts get maximum attention.
  • Audit what lands on the default route; a healthy target is ~95% of alerts matching specific routes.

8. Heartbeat monitoring: watch the watchers#8-heartbeat-monitoring-watch-the-watchers

Heartbeats are monitoring for your monitoring — a dead man's switch. IRM expects a periodic ping; if none arrives within the configured timeout (1 minute – 24 hours), it creates an alert group and escalates. When the heartbeat resumes, the alert auto-resolves.

  • Configure via the integration's More options → Heartbeat Settings; copy the heartbeat URL into the monitoring system.
  • Set the timeout longer than the monitoring interval to avoid false alerts.
  • Supported types: Alertmanager, Webhook/Formatted Webhook, ElastAlert, PRTG, Zabbix (if Heartbeat Settings isn't in the menu, the type doesn't support it). Workaround: a dedicated Webhook integration used purely as a heartbeat receiver.
  • Prometheus pattern: an always-firing rule (expr: vector(1)) routed to a heartbeat receiver with group_wait: 0s, group_interval: 1m, repeat_interval: 50s, send_resolved: false.
  • Enable for every integration where silence indicates a problem.

9. Maintenance mode#9-maintenance-mode

Suppress noise deliberately during planned work — integration page → More options → Start Maintenance:

ModeBehaviorUse for
Silence escalationsAlerts created, escalations pausedTesting integrations and templates
Group alertsEverything into one maintenance group, no escalationPlanned maintenance windows
Disable alertsPayloads ignored entirelyMajor changes

Always set a duration (it auto-disables), notify the team, and review the accumulated maintenance group afterwards.

10. Testing#10-testing

Send demo alert

verifies connectivity only — it does not test grouping, templates against real payloads, or the full ingestion pipeline. For real validation:

  1. Send actual HTTP requests (cURL or the source tool's test button) with production-shaped payloads.
  2. Repeat with the same grouping key (alert_uid for formatted webhooks) to verify grouping.
  3. Confirm the alert lands on the intended route and the escalation chain fires correctly.
  4. For Grafana Alerting, run an end-to-end test: alert rule → contact point → integration → route → chain → notification.

11. Integrations as code (Terraform and API)#11-integrations-as-code-terraform-and-api

Terraform

grafana_oncall_integration creates the integration; grafana_oncall_route adds routes. The integration's read-only link attribute exposes the endpoint URL for configuring the source tool.

terraform
resource "grafana_oncall_integration" "integration_with_templates" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  name     = "integration_with_templates"
  type     = "webhook"
  team_id  = data.grafana_oncall_team.my_team.id

  default_route {
    escalation_chain_id = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  }

  templates {
    grouping_key = "{{ payload.group_id }}"
    slack {
      title     = "Slack title"
      message   = <<-EOT
          This is example of multiline template
          {{ payload.message }}
        EOT
      image_url = "{{ payload.image_url }}"
    }
  }
}

resource "grafana_oncall_route" "example_route" {
  integration_id      = grafana_oncall_integration.integration_with_templates.id
  escalation_chain_id = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.critical.id
  routing_type        = "jinja2"
  routing_regex       = "{{ payload.severity == 'critical' }}"
  position            = 0

  slack {
    channel_id = data.grafana_oncall_slack_channel.alerts.slack_id
    enabled    = true
  }
}

Key details:

  • type values include grafana_alerting, alertmanager, webhook, formatted_webhook, datadog, zabbix, newrelic, sentry, amazon_sns, inbound_email, direct_paging, and more.
  • default_route is required (exactly one block); it takes escalation_chain_id plus optional slack/telegram/msteams blocks.
  • The templates block covers grouping_key, resolve_signal, acknowledge_signal, source_link, and per-channel appearance blocks (web, slack, telegram, microsoft_teams, email, mobile_app, sms, phone_call).
  • Routes: position starts at 0; set routing_type = "jinja2" and put the Jinja2 condition in routing_regex (regex is the deprecated default).
  • Import existing resources: terraform import grafana_oncall_integration.name "{{ id }}".

API

Auth: Authorization: Bearer <token> plus X-Grafana-URL: https://your-stack.grafana.net. Permissions: grafana-irm-app.integrations:read / :write.

  • Integrations: GET/POST {{API_URL}}/api/v1/integrations/, GET/PUT/DELETE .../<ID>/. Create body: name, type (required), team_id, templates, default_route, labels, security (["bearer_token"]).
  • Maintenance: POST .../integrations/<ID>/maintenance_start (mode: silence_escalations | group_alerts | disable_alerts; duration in seconds) and .../maintenance_stop.
  • Routes: GET/POST {{API_URL}}/api/v1/routes/ (params: integration_id, escalation_chain_id, routing_type, routing_regex, position — -1 appends), GET/PUT/DELETE .../<ID>/.

As-code best practices

  • Module per team: one module creating the team's integration, default route, severity routes, and chains together — new team onboarding becomes one module call.
  • Expose the link output so source-tool configuration (Alertmanager receivers, Datadog webhooks) can consume the endpoint URL from Terraform state instead of copy-paste.
  • Keep integrations, routes, chains, and schedules in one workspace — terraform plan shows the full alerting path for any change.
  • Manage direct paging as code too: enable manually_manage_direct_paging_integrations in IRM settings if you want team direct-paging integrations under Terraform control.
  • Git + PR review + CI: terraform plan on every PR; apply on merge; send a real test alert after applying integration or template changes.
  • RBAC on tokens: least-privilege service accounts; separate tokens for CI vs alert ingestion.

12. Quick reference checklist#12-quick-reference-checklist

  • One integration per team; source-side label selectors scope the traffic
  • Every integration named clearly and assigned to a team

  • Templates add runbooks, dashboards, and severity context; tested with real payloads

Incident Management

A practical guide to declaring, coordinating, resolving, and learning from incidents with Grafana Cloud IRM. Written for platform teams configuring incident response and for the responders who run incidents. Part 5 of the IRM enablement series, following On-Call Schedules, Escalation Chains, Integrations, and Insights.

1. Where incidents fit#1-where-incidents-fit

Grafana IRM brings together two capabilities: OnCall (alert grouping, escalation, schedules, paging) and Incident (the formal incident lifecycle — coordination, timeline, retrospectives). Incidents are the coordination layer *above* alert groups.

text
Alert fires → Alert group (OnCall) → Incident declared → Coordinate → Resolve → Review

Incident management lives under Alerts & IRM → IRM → Incidents; its configuration is under IRM → Settings → Incidents.

Incident vs alert group — know the difference:

AspectAlert groupIncident
CreationAutomatic from alertsManual or via escalation step
PurposeGroup related alertsCoordinate response to a significant event
ScopeTechnical signalsBusiness impact and coordination
LifecycleFiring → Acknowledged → ResolvedCustomizable status progression
CommunicationNotifications to respondersStakeholder updates, dedicated channels

Not every alert deserves an incident. Declare an incident when an event needs coordinated cross-team response, formal or compliance tracking, stakeholder communication, or a post-incident review. Attach the contributing alert groups (up to 5 per incident) to preserve technical context.

2. Declaring an incident#2-declaring-an-incident

Incidents can be declared from wherever the signal first appears:

  • IRM UI — Incidents → Declare incident. Fields: Title (required), Severity, Labels; "More options" reveals Channel prefix, Status, and Start time.
  • Dashboard panel — panel menu → More → Declare incident, so you can declare while looking at the concerning metric.
  • From an alert group — Alert groups → select → Actions → Declare incident.
  • From a firing alert in Grafana Alerting — the Declare incident button on the alert.
  • From Slack — /grafana incident new <severity> <title> opens a modal; submitting creates the incident and its channel.
  • Automatically from an escalation chain — add a Declare incident step (note: only works on non-default routes; configure a specific route for alerts that should auto-declare).
  • API / prefill — the IncidentsService.CreateIncident RPC, plus URL-parameter prefill and a browser bookmarklet.
What declaration triggers

(depending on your integrations): a dedicated Slack channel, notification of relevant responders, generation of the incident timeline, optional conference bridges (Zoom), and optional tickets in external systems (Jira). Title, severity, labels, and status stay editable throughout.

Private incidents

tick "Private incident" for access-restricted coordination — the Slack channel is set private and only the creator sees it until others are added via the Roles panel or invited to the channel. (IRM can't auto-create private Slack channels, so create one manually and invite the Grafana Cloud app first.)

3. Severities, statuses, and workflow#3-severities-statuses-and-workflow

Pre-configured severities:

SeverityMeaning
CriticalUrgently requires immediate attention
MajorSignificant, blocking problem that requires help
MinorMay be affecting customers, but no one is blocked
PendingSeverity to be decided — determine as soon as possible

The default severity on declaration is Pending (configurable). Custom severities carry a Label, a numeric Level (1 = highest), and optional description/icon — note the Level is immutable after creation, so to re-rank, create a new severity and archive the old one. Design severities around your SLAs and team capacity, and map any priority scheme onto them (P1→Critical, P2→Major, P3→Minor, P4→Pending).

Statuses

default to Active and Resolved. You can add custom statuses (name, description, icon, color) that reflect your real process — a common progression teams build is Declared → Acknowledged → Mitigated → Resolved → Closed. The managed-incident workflow stages are Declare → Document → Collaborate → Resolve.

Custom fields

add incident metadata of type Text, Number, Single-select, or Multi-select. Keep the set small, prefer selects for consistency, and mark a field Mandatory only when it truly must be completed before resolution.

4. Roles, participants, and tasks#4-roles-participants-and-tasks

Roles.

Two key roles ship by default: Commander (oversees the incident — communication, tasks, updates) and Investigator (diagnoses and resolves). Add custom roles as needed, flagging the critical ones as key roles. Assign roles from the incident's Roles panel, or in Slack with the Assume role button or /grafana incident assign <role> <user>.

Participants.

Invite individual users or whole teams; invitations go through the Direct Paging integration and reach people via their personal notification rules. Choose Default or Important priority. Participant responses matter: Accepting or Declining resolves the associated alert group and stops escalation; Dismissing leaves it active. Status icons: 🔔 invited, 👁️ acknowledged, ✔️ accepted, ❌ declined.

Tasks / action items.

Track work in the Tasks sidebar (+Task) or Slack (/grafana incident tasks add). Each task is assignable and cycles Todo → In Progress → Done. Tasks can be converted to GitHub or Jira issues (status syncs back), keeping incident coordination and engineering tracking aligned.

5. The timeline: your single source of truth#5-the-timeline-your-single-source-of-truth

The incident timeline is a timestamped chronological record. Add status updates, notes (Markdown; URLs auto-collected into "Links and context"), images/screenshots (PNG/JPG; Cmd/Ctrl+I captures a Grafana screenshot), queries (captured as static snapshots), and dashboard panels.

Slack emoji reactions feed the timeline directly:

  • 🤖 (:robot_face:) — add a message to the timeline
  • 💡 (:bulb:) — create a task / post-incident action item
  • 📢 (:loudspeaker:) — add a status update

Filter the timeline by importance (Highly relevant / All relevant / All activity), time range, or reaction, and search it. Tagging key decisions and actions as you go is what makes the retrospective fast and honest.

Status updates

are distinct from notes: they're the stakeholder-facing milestone communication (impact confirmed, mitigation in progress, resolved). Add them in the UI, via /grafana incident update add, or the 📢 reaction. You can't add status updates to an already-resolved incident, so communicate resolution before closing.

6. ChatOps: running incidents from Slack#6-chatops-running-incidents-from-slack

Install the Grafana Cloud Slack app and have each responder link their Slack profile (Profile → IRM → Slack connection). Configure incident channel behavior under IRM → Integrations → Apps → Slack:

  • Auto-create a channel per incident (the war room), auto-invite users with active roles, and post updates there.
  • Channel name template defaults to {{ prefix }}-{{ date }}-{{ title }} → incident-2024-01-12-database-outage. Variables: prefix, title, date, id. Template changes aren't retroactive.
  • Auto-archive resolved channels after a configurable number of days (30–60 recommended), with a warning posted 24 hours before.

Type /grafana in a channel for context-aware action buttons. Common commands: incident new, incident drill, incident assign/unassign, incident severity, incident update add, incident tasks add, incident resolve <summary>, escalate <user>.

Incident announcements

auto-post incident info (severity, status, roles, labels) to designated channels on declaration and keep it updated. Each announcement channel takes an incident filter using incident query syntax (e.g. severity:critical → #incidents-critical), so you can route by severity, team, or service and separate drills from production. Incident briefings post a custom message (runbooks, checklists, escalation contacts) into a new incident's channel.

Microsoft Teams is supported and managed through the UI; confirm Teams war-room parity directly if your teams use it, as the incident-specific Teams behavior is lightly documented.

7. Labels, service_name, and the Service Center#7-labels-service-name-and-the-service-center

Labels are the connective tissue between incidents and analytics. They arrive three ways: static (set at the integration, applied to all its incidents), dynamic (transferred from the alert group on declaration), and manual (added during the incident). The flow is Alert rule labels → Alert group labels → Incident labels.

Essential labels: service_name (required for Service Center), severity, team, environment. service_name is the linchpin — it flows from alert to alert group to incident, associates incidents with services and SLOs, and powers the Service Center's unified per-service view (on-call handoffs, operational reviews, SLO correlation). Apply it consistently and preserve it through label templates, and per-service incident analytics come for free (see the Insights guide).

8. Resolve and learn#8-resolve-and-learn

Resolving well is what turns an incident into an improvement:

  • Add resolution notes capturing root cause, resolution steps, and lessons learned (/grafana incident resolve <summary> records a resolution summary).
  • Complete the record: finalize the timeline, attach all relevant alert groups, and correct labels — accurate labels mean accurate analytics.
  • Mandatory custom fields must be completed before the incident can be resolved.
  • Review the metrics: MTTR (mean time to resolve) and MTTA (mean time to acknowledge) from IRM Insights, service health against SLOs, and recurring-issue trends.
  • Feed learnings back into the loop: tune alert quality and thresholds, improve escalation chains, update runbooks based on resolution patterns, and add monitoring for earlier detection.

Because Insights reads MTTR from the incident's start and end timestamps, correct those timestamps during the review — the analytics recalculate automatically.

9. Practice with drills#9-practice-with-drills

Run drill incidents to rehearse response without touching production metrics — drills are excluded from dashboards and analytics. Declare one with + Start a drill in the UI, /grafana incident drill <title> in Slack, or isDrill: true via the API. Separate drill and production announcement channels, and run drills regularly to keep the team fluent and validate that integrations still fire.

10. Incident management as code#10-incident-management-as-code

Incident *configuration* — severities, statuses, roles, custom fields, labels, and Slack settings — is managed through the IRM UI or the Incident API, not Terraform. There is no grafana_incident Terraform resource. What *is* codifiable is the automation that declares incidents: the Declare incident escalation step lives on routes and escalation chains, which are Terraform-manageable (grafana_oncall_escalation, grafana_oncall_escalation_chain) — so you can codify *when* incidents get declared even though the incident config itself is UI/API.

The Incident API

A JSON/HTTP RPC API (not REST): every call is an HTTP POST to {base}/{Service}.{Method}.

  • Base: https://your-stack.grafana.net/api/plugins/grafana-irm-app/resources/api/v1/
  • Auth: Authorization: Bearer glsa_... (service account token), Content-Type: application/json
  • Response: every response has an error string (empty = success); lists paginate via a cursor.
  • Official clients: Go (github.com/grafana/incident-go), Node (@grafana/incident-node).

Declare an incident with IncidentsService.CreateIncident:

bash
curl -X POST \
  "https://your-stack.grafana.net/api/plugins/grafana-irm-app/resources/api/v1/IncidentsService.CreateIncident" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $GRAFANA_SA_TOKEN" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "title": "Checkout latency elevated",
    "severity": "major",
    "status": "active",
    "labels": [{ "label": "service_name", "value": "checkout" }],
    "roomPrefix": "inc",
    "isDrill": false
  }'

Severity options are lowercase (pending, minor, major, critical); status is active or resolved (create as resolved to open a retrospective incident).

Nine services cover the surface: IncidentsService (query/get/declare/update, assign roles, update labels), ActivityService (timeline entries — AddActivity, body up to 65,536 chars), TasksService, RolesService, StatusService, FieldsService (custom fields *and* labels, modeled as a "labels" domain), KeyUpdatesService, IntegrationService, and UsersService.

As-code best practices

  • Codify the trigger, configure the rest via API scripts. Manage declare-incident escalation steps in Terraform alongside your chains; script severities, statuses, roles, custom fields, and labels through the Fields/Status/Roles services so incident config is reproducible and reviewable even though it isn't Terraform-native.
  • Version your incident config scripts in Git with PR review, the same discipline as the rest of the series.
  • Use service account tokens with least privilege, and remember IRM user IDs differ from Grafana user IDs (map via the grafana_oncall_user data source when wiring OnCall pieces).
  • Automate post-incident hygiene where you can — e.g. a webhook or scheduled job that flags incidents missing service_name or resolution notes.

11. Quick reference checklist#11-quick-reference-checklist

  • Severities mapped to your SLAs; default severity set intentionally
  • Statuses reflect your real workflow (e.g. Declared → Mitigated → Resolved → Closed)
  • Commander and Investigator roles understood; assignment habit established
  • Declare-incident escalation step on critical, non-default routes
  • Slack app installed, users linked, auto-channel + announcements configured
  • Channel naming template and auto-archive (30–60 days) set

Tasks

A practical guide to coordinating action items during and after an incident with Grafana Cloud IRM Tasks — creation, ownership, status tracking, GitHub/Jira follow-through, and the API. Part of the IRM enablement series, closely tied to Incident Management.

1. What Tasks are#1-what-tasks-are

Tasks are Grafana IRM's built-in action-item tracker for incidents. They help you organize, assign, and track the specific steps needed to resolve an incident, so responders can be sure nothing falls through the cracks. Each task has a short description, a single assignee, and a status.

They live in the Tasks section of the right-hand sidebar on the incident details page, and can be created and managed from within the incident's Slack channel. Alongside the timeline, tasks form the permanent record of what was done during an incident — and the mechanism for carrying remediation work forward afterward.

Use tasks to track remediation steps, assign responsibility, monitor progress, and record the actions taken. Converting tasks to GitHub or Jira issues carries the durable follow-up work into your engineering backlog.

2. Creating tasks#2-creating-tasks

There are several entry points, so you can capture an action item wherever the conversation is happening:

  • Tasks sidebar — type an action-oriented description in the Tasks field and click +Task.
  • Slack slash command — /grafana incident tasks add <task>, or assign at creation with /grafana incident tasks add <task> @user.
  • Slack action button — type /grafana in the incident channel and click Add task.
  • 💡 bulb emoji — react to any message in the incident Slack channel with 💡 (:bulb:) to turn it into a task (a post-incident-review action item) without leaving the conversation.
  • Paste a GitHub issue URL — paste an issue link into the Tasks field and click +Task; it appears as a task with its status synced from GitHub.

A task carries a description (text), an optional single assignee, and a status. There is no separate due-date or priority field, so keep the description specific and self-contained.

3. Statuses, assignment, and lifecycle#3-statuses-assignment-and-lifecycle

Tasks move through three statuses. Click the status icon to cycle it forward:

StatusIconMeaning
Todo☐Created, but work hasn't started
In Progress🕜Work has begun but isn't complete
Done✔️Completed

The icon cycles Todo → In Progress → Done → Todo. Assign a task by clicking the + people icon and selecting a user; reassign or unassign by clicking the current assignee. Every task has exactly one assignee — that single-owner model is deliberate and worth preserving.

Some tasks are system-created (for example, role-assignment tasks) and are marked immutable — they can't be edited or reassigned.

4. GitHub and Jira follow-through#4-github-and-jira-follow-through

An incident ends, but the remediation work often doesn't. Converting tasks to issues moves that work into the tools your engineers already live in, and keeps it alive after the incident channel is archived.

GitHub Issues.

From a task's menu (⋯), choose Convert to GitHub Issue, enter organization/repository, add a description, and confirm — the task now links to the issue and shows its live GitHub status. You can also Link to an existing issue, or create a task by pasting an issue URL. Unlinking only removes the issue from the incident's task list; it never modifies or deletes the GitHub issue itself. Setup needs the IRM Admin role plus GitHub admin rights to install the Grafana IRM GitHub app and pick which repositories to connect.

Jira.

From a task's menu, Convert to Jira Issue (or Link to an existing one). Jira status syncs with the issue, and the Jira integration can auto-create an issue when an incident is declared and update its status as the incident progresses. Two constraints to know: it's Jira Cloud only, and IRM sends only four fields on creation — Summary, Description, Project, and Issue Type — so issue creation will fail if your Jira project requires other mandatory fields. Trim those required fields or pre-fill them in Jira.

5. Capturing action items in the moment#5-capturing-action-items-in-the-moment

The best incident notes are the ones you don't have to reconstruct afterward. During the incident:

  • Use the 💡 reaction in Slack to capture decisions and follow-ups as tasks the instant they're mentioned — no app switching, no "we'll write it down later."
  • Assign an owner at creation (/grafana incident tasks add "deploy hotfix" @bob) so accountability is set immediately.
  • Keep the incident commander's view current by updating statuses as work progresses — status is how the whole room reads overall progress.
  • Use /grafana incident tasks list to review the open task list at any point, especially before moving to resolve the incident.

6. Post-incident follow-through#6-post-incident-follow-through

Tasks bridge the live incident and the retrospective. Action items captured with 💡 become the raw material of the post-incident review. Before you resolve an incident, review the task list so nothing open is silently lost when the channel archives (the incident Slack channel auto-archives after a configurable window — commonly 30–60 days — with a 24-hour warning). For anything that outlives the incident, convert it to a GitHub or Jira issue so it lands in a durable backlog rather than a channel that will disappear.

7. Best practices#7-best-practices

  • Write clear, actionable descriptions. "Restart the database cluster in us-west" beats "Fix database." A task should read like a checklist item someone can pick up cold.
  • Assign a single owner. One assignee per task is the model — use it to make accountability unambiguous. Shared ownership is no ownership.
  • Update statuses in real time. The commander and the room read progress from task status; stale statuses hide risk.
  • Capture in the moment. Use 💡 in Slack to log action items as they're discussed, not after.
  • Keep tasks atomic. Break multi-step work into separate tasks rather than one sprawling item.
  • Review before resolving. Walk the open task list before closing the incident; nothing important should be left in Todo.
  • Push durable work to an issue tracker. Convert follow-ups to GitHub/Jira so they survive channel archival and enter the normal engineering workflow.

8. Roles and permissions#8-roles-and-permissions

Tasks use basic-role authorization, not RBAC — the same as the rest of incident management. Creating, editing, and assigning tasks requires the Editor or Admin basic role; Viewers have read-only access. Setting up the GitHub or Jira integrations additionally requires the IRM Admin role plus admin rights in the respective external tool. RBAC roles don't change task permissions, so plan access at the basic-role level for responders who need to manage tasks.

9. Tasks as code (the Incident API)#9-tasks-as-code-the-incident-api

There is no Terraform resource for incident tasks — incidents and their tasks are managed only through the Incident API. That said, tasks are fully scriptable, which is useful for automations like seeding a standard checklist of tasks onto every incident of a given type.

The Incident API is JSON-over-HTTP RPC: every call is an HTTP POST to {base}/{Service}.{Method}.

  • Base: https://your-stack.grafana.net/api/plugins/grafana-irm-app/resources/api/v1
  • Auth: Authorization: Bearer glsa_... (a Grafana service account token)
  • Content-Type: application/json; charset=utf-8

TasksService exposes five methods:

MethodKey fieldsPurpose
AddTaskincidentID, text, assignToUserIDCreate a task
UpdateTaskStatusincidentID, taskID, status (todo/progress/done)Move a task's status
UpdateTaskTextincidentID, taskID, textEdit the description
UpdateTaskUserincidentID, taskID, userIDReassign the task
DeleteTaskincidentID, taskIDRemove the task

Note the surface: assignment is UpdateTaskUser (there is no AssignTask), removal is DeleteTask, and there's no standalone task-query RPC — tasks come back embedded in each response's taskList (which also carries todoCount and doneCount) and via incident queries.

Create a task:

bash
curl "https://your-stack.grafana.net/api/plugins/grafana-irm-app/resources/api/v1/TasksService.AddTask" \
  --request POST \
  --header 'Content-Type: application/json; charset=utf-8' \
  --header 'Authorization: Bearer glsa_...' \
  --data '{
    "incidentID": "incident-123",
    "text": "Restart database cluster in us-west region",
    "assignToUserID": "user-456"
  }'

Move it to done:

bash
curl "https://your-stack.grafana.net/api/plugins/grafana-irm-app/resources/api/v1/TasksService.UpdateTaskStatus" \
  --request POST \
  --header 'Content-Type: application/json; charset=utf-8' \
  --header 'Authorization: Bearer glsa_...' \
  --data '{"incidentID":"incident-123","taskID":"task-789","status":"done"}'

An official Go client (github.com/grafana/incident-go) is available, and outgoing webhooks emit task activity kinds — taskAdded, taskUpdated, taskCompleted, taskDeleted — which you can use to mirror tasks into external systems.

As-code best practices

  • Seed standard checklists. Script AddTask calls to stamp a consistent set of action items onto incidents of a given type or severity (e.g. "post status page update", "notify account team").
  • Mirror with webhooks. Use the task* activity kinds to sync tasks outward to a system of record if you need one beyond GitHub/Jira.
  • Least-privilege tokens. Use a scoped service account token for task automation, and keep the automation code in Git with review.

10. Quick reference checklist#10-quick-reference-checklist

  • Responders who manage tasks have Editor or Admin basic roles
  • GitHub and/or Jira integration installed (Admin role) if you want follow-through sync
  • Team knows the 💡 Slack reaction captures a task in the moment
  • Task descriptions are specific and actionable
  • Every task has a single owner assigned
  • Statuses updated in real time during the incident
  • Open task list reviewed before resolving the incident
  • Durable follow-ups converted to GitHub/Jira issues before channel archival
  • Standard task checklists seeded via the API for recurring incident types (optional)

Insights

A practical guide to measuring and improving incident response with Grafana Cloud IRM Insights — alert group metrics, incident analytics, insight logs, and the improvement loops they enable. Part 4 of the IRM enablement series, following On-Call Schedules, Escalation Chains, and Integrations.

1. What IRM Insights is#1-what-irm-insights-is

IRM Insights is the analytics layer of Grafana IRM, found under Alerts & IRM → IRM → Insights. It has two halves:

  • Alert group insights and metrics — Prometheus-format metrics plus audit-style insight logs covering alert volume, response and resolution times, notification load per user, and configuration changes.
  • Incident insights — a pre-built dashboard plus a Grafana Incident data source for building custom dashboards over incident data: MTTR, MTTD, frequency, team impact, and label-based patterns.

Questions it answers: Where are our response bottlenecks? Which integrations are noisiest? Who is carrying too much on-call load? Are MTTR and MTTA trending the right way? Who changed IRM configuration and when?

Incident insights are included with all Grafana IRM plans at no additional cost.

2. The four alert group metrics#2-the-four-alert-group-metrics

IRM exports four Prometheus metrics. In Grafana Cloud they live in the preinstalled grafanacloud-usage data source (org-level — one data source serves all stacks, so filter by stack id or slug). Cloud metric prefix: grafanacloud_oncall_instance_; self-managed OSS prefix: oncall_.

MetricTypeWhat it measures
..._alert_groups_totalGaugeAlert groups per integration by state (firing, acknowledged, resolved, silenced)
..._alert_groups_response_time_secondsHistogramTime from alert group start to first action (MTTA proxy), rolling last 7 days
..._alert_groups_resolution_time_secondsHistogramTime from alert group start to resolution (MTTR proxy), rolling last 7 days
..._user_was_notified_of_alert_groups_totalCounterAlert groups each user was notified of (on-call load)

Labels: id/slug/org_id (stack), team, integration, service_name (from the alert group's service_name label — "No service" if absent), state (on the gauge), username (on the notification counter). Histogram buckets: 60, 300, 600, 3600, +Inf seconds.

Example queries from the docs:

promql
# Firing alert groups for one integration
grafanacloud_oncall_instance_alert_groups_total{integration="Grafana Alerting", state="firing"}

# Alert groups responded to within 10 minutes
grafanacloud_oncall_instance_alert_groups_response_time_seconds_bucket{integration="Grafana Alerting", le="600"}

# Per-user notification load
sum by (username) (grafanacloud_oncall_instance_user_was_notified_of_alert_groups_total)

# Notification rate for one service
rate(grafanacloud_oncall_instance_user_was_notified_of_alert_groups_total{service_name="auth"}[5m])

Note: incident data is *not* exported as Prometheus metrics — query it through the Grafana Incident data source (section 4).

3. The pre-built dashboards#3-the-pre-built-dashboards

Alert Groups Insights dashboard

(IRM plugin): find it in the dashboards list under folder General, tag irm. Select grafanacloud_usage as the data source; filter by stack, team, and integration.

Two operational cautions:

  • Plugin updates and re-imports reset customizations. Preserve changes with Save As — or better, export the JSON and manage your copy as code (section 7).
  • Re-import when needed via Administration → Plugins → IRM → Dashboards → Re-import.
Incident Insights dashboard

first-time setup via IRM → Insights → Incidents tab → "Set up Insights dashboard". Setup auto-configures the Grafana Incident data source and creates a service account with a viewer token, then builds the dashboard.

Core incident metrics:

  • MTTR = incidentEnd − incidentStart (falls back to closedTime if end unset)
  • MTTD = incidentCreated − incidentStart (exposed as time_to_discovery)
  • Frequency, team impact, root-cause patterns via labels, time-of-day patterns

Timestamps incidentStart and incidentEnd are editable — even after resolution — and MTTR/MTTD recalculate automatically. Correct your timelines in the post-incident review and your analytics stay honest. Insights queries ignore Drill (practice) incidents unless you explicitly query isdrill:true.

4. Custom dashboards#4-custom-dashboards

On alert group metrics

any panel against the grafanacloud-usage data source using the PromQL above. Useful panels: noisy-integration ranking (sum by (integration) (alert_groups_total{state="firing"})), per-service response-time heatmap, per-user notification load.

On incident data

create panels against the Grafana Incident data source using incident query syntax. Dashboard template variables must use these reserved, case-sensitive names: statuses (Status query type), severities (Severity), labels (Label), roles (Role). Combined example:

text
or(status:$statuses) or(severity:$severities) or(label:$labels)

Filter logic gotcha: multiple label: filters combine with AND; other filters like status: combine with OR.

Docs-recommended practices: start with the pre-built dashboards, create custom dashboards per team or service, use consistent labeling, track trends over time rather than point-in-time values, and read MTTR alongside incident count (a falling MTTR with rising count is not a win).

5. Meta-monitoring: alert on your alerting#5-meta-monitoring-alert-on-your-alerting

Insights metrics are ordinary Prometheus series — alert on them like anything else (Admin or Editor role required):

  • Notification failures (Grafana Alerting side): grafanacloud_grafana_instance_alerting_notifications_failed_total:rate5m (labeled by integration).
  • Silence-means-trouble detection: enable per-integration heartbeat monitoring (timeout 1 minute – 24 hours, set longer than the monitoring interval) — covered in the Integrations guide.
  • On-call coverage: enable schedule gap and empty-shift reports; schedule quality metrics (coverage percentage, balance score, overloaded users) are in-product only, not exported.
  • Response degradation: alert when the share of alert groups responded to within 10 minutes (le="600" bucket over _count) drops below your target.
  • Alert state history is queryable in Loki: data source grafanacloud-<stack>-alert-state-history, query {from="state-history"} | json.

6. Insight logs: the audit trail#6-insight-logs-the-audit-trail

Alert group insight logs record configuration changes and system events. In Grafana Cloud they're auto-provisioned in the Usage Insights Loki data source (grafanacloud-<YOUR-STACK-NAME>-usage-insights); storage and queries don't count against your Loki costs.

Base query:

logql
{instance_type="oncall"} | logfmt | __error__=``

Three log types via the action_type label:

  1. resource — created/updated/deleted for IRM resources (integrations, escalation chains and policies, schedules and shifts, webhooks, API tokens, users…), with author, resource_type, and JSON prev_state/new_state.
  2. maintenance — maintenance started/finished per integration, with the mode used.
  3. chat_ops — Slack/Teams/Telegram/mobile-app connection changes.

Examples from the docs:

logql
# Everything one user changed
{instance_type="oncall"} | logfmt | __error__=`` | action_type = `resource` and author="username"

# All schedule changes
{instance_type="oncall"} | logfmt | __error__=`` | action_type = `resource` and (resource_type=`web_schedule` or resource_type=`calendar_schedule` or resource_type=`ical_schedule`)

# Maintenance history for an integration
{instance_type="oncall"} | logfmt | __error__=`` | action_type = `maintenance` and resource_id=`INTEGRATION_ID`

Resource IDs are visible in IRM URLs (e.g. .../integrations/C5VXMIFKKP67K). Use insight logs to answer "who changed this escalation chain before the missed page?" — and pair them with as-code management so the same question is also answerable from Git history.

7. The improvement loops#7-the-improvement-loops

Insights only pay off when wired into recurring practices:

On-call load balancing (weekly/monthly).

Review sum by (username) notification load and schedule balance scores; rebalance rotations for overloaded members; account for holidays and follow-the-sun distribution.

Noise reduction (monthly).

Rank integrations by firing alert groups; fix grouping keys and thresholds on the top offenders; add volume-throttling escalation steps (e.g. only page when >5 alerts in 30 minutes); verify maintenance mode is used for planned work.

Response tuning (monthly/quarterly).

Watch time to first acknowledgment, escalation depth, and false escalations — slow acknowledgment argues for earlier important-notification steps; deep escalations argue for shorter waits or more channels; false escalations argue for alert-quality work or throttling.

Post-incident hygiene (every incident).

Finalize the timeline (edit incidentStart/incidentEnd so MTTR/MTTD are accurate), add resolution notes, attach related alert groups (max 5 per incident), and correct labels — analytics are only as good as this discipline.

Labeling for analytics (ongoing).

Labels serve routing, triage, *and* analytics. A minimal effective set is 4–5 labels — Grafana Engineering uses namespace, severity, service_name, cluster. service_name is the key that ties alert rules → alert groups → incidents → SLOs together in the Service Center. Limits: 50 labels per alert group (first 50 alphabetically win), and labels are immutable once assigned to an alert group.

8. Insights as code (Terraform and API)#8-insights-as-code-terraform-and-api

Insights itself is mostly read-side, but everything that *feeds* it — and everything you build *on* it — can be managed as code:

Dashboards as code.

Export the pre-built Alert Groups Insights dashboard JSON (Save As → export) and provision your customized copy with the standard grafana_dashboard resource. This also solves the plugin-update-resets-customizations problem permanently:

terraform
resource "grafana_dashboard" "irm_insights_custom" {
  folder      = grafana_folder.observability.id
  config_json = file("${path.module}/dashboards/irm-insights-custom.json")
}
Meta-monitoring alerts as code.

Provision alert rules on the IRM metrics with grafana_rule_group, pointing at the grafanacloud-usage data source — e.g. a rule on per-user notification load or on the le="600" response-time bucket share.

Labels as code.

Attach analytics labels to integrations in Terraform so service_name and friends flow into every metric:

terraform
data "grafana_oncall_label" "service_payments" {
  key   = "service_name"
  value = "payments"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_integration" "payments" {
  name    = "Payments Alertmanager"
  type    = "alertmanager"
  labels  = [data.grafana_oncall_label.service_payments]
  default_route {
    escalation_chain_id = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.default.id
  }
}

(Dynamic labels are UI/API only.)

Provider setup

is the same as the rest of the series: Grafana provider >= 3.0.0 with the oncall alias. Incident resources are managed via the separate Incident API, not the OnCall API; ChatOps configs and admin settings are not IaC-manageable. Note that IRM user IDs differ from Grafana user IDs — map via data "grafana_oncall_user".

As-code best practices

keep dashboards, alert rules, labels, and the IRM resources they measure in one workspace; PR review plus terraform plan in CI; service-account tokens with least privilege (viewer for dashboard data sources); and treat the insight-log audit trail and Git history as complementary records of change.

9. Service Center and SLOs#9-service-center-and-slos

The service_name label is the connective tissue: it flows from alert rule → alert instance → alert group → incident, appears on all four IRM metrics, and powers the Service Center — a per-service view unifying health, SLOs, alerts, incidents, on-call, and synthetic checks. Label consistently and per-service insights come free.

Settings

A practical guide to administering Grafana Cloud IRM — access and roles, API tokens, ChatOps, org-level behavior toggles, and incident-response configuration — and to managing as much of it as code as the platform allows. Written for the platform admins who own an IRM stack. Part of the IRM enablement series.

1. What the Settings section is#1-what-the-settings-section-is

The IRM Settings area is the administration hub for an IRM stack, at Alerts & IRM → IRM → Settings. It's a tabbed page with three documented tabs:

  • Admin & API — org-wide behavior toggles, API token management, and the IRM API base URLs.
  • Incidents — the configurable incident-response workflow: severities, statuses, roles, custom fields, and channel prefixes.
  • Team Access Management — per-team visibility and the default team.

One scoping subtlety worth knowing up front: not everything an admin configures lives under the Settings tab. Several org-level behaviors sit under IRM → Integrations → Apps → Slack (incident channel creation, auto-archive, acknowledge reminders), and connecting a chat workspace is done through the Grafana Cloud portal. So "Settings" in the strict sense is those three tabs; "settings" in the everyday admin sense spans those plus the Slack app config and ChatOps connection. This guide covers the whole administrative surface.

2. Access management: roles#2-access-management-roles

Access is layered: basic roles (inherited from Grafana) plus IRM RBAC roles (fine-grained, and they don't change the basic role).

Basic roles:

RoleIRM access
ViewerRead-only across IRM
EditorEdit most resources (schedules, alert groups); cannot create integrations, escalation chains, or outgoing webhooks
AdminFull access to all IRM features and settings
NoneNo default access; grant access with an IRM RBAC role
Key IRM RBAC roles

(assigning any of them also grants IRM plugin access):

  • OnCaller — read all IRM plus edit alert groups, schedules, and own settings. The role for on-call responders.
  • Notifications Receiver — receive alert notifications and edit own IRM user settings; the minimal responder grant.
  • Incident Access — plugin access for incident workflows only, no OnCall permissions.
  • Reader / Editor / Admin — map to the basic roles.
  • Specialized reader/editor roles for each resource type: Integrations, Escalation Chains, Schedules, ChatOps, Outgoing Webhooks, Maintenance, API Keys, Notification Settings, User Settings (Reader/Editor/Admin), and org Settings (Reader/Editor).

Permission strings are prefixed grafana-irm-app. — e.g. grafana-irm-app.schedules:write, grafana-irm-app.other-settings:write, grafana-irm-app.api-keys:write.

Assign roles

as an org admin: Administration → Users and access → Users → select the user → Role picker → under Grafana IRM pick the role → Apply. Roles can also be assigned to teams and via the API/Terraform.

Features that don't support RBAC.

Incidents, Tasks, and Incident settings still use basic-role authorization only — RBAC roles don't affect them. Plan for this: managing incident configuration requires the Admin basic role, regardless of RBAC.

3. Teams: scoping resources#3-teams-scoping-resources

IRM teams sync from Grafana teams (Administration → Teams) roughly every four hours, and immediately when you open or refresh any IRM page. Set team visibility under Settings → Team Access Management — either all Grafana users can see the team's resources, or only team members and admins. Admins see all teams; Editors and Viewers see only teams they belong to (unless visibility is "all users"). Cross-team resources appear to non-members as 🔒 Private resource. Note that to *manage* a team's resources in IRM you must be a member of that team — even admins add themselves temporarily.

RBAC decides *what actions* a user can take; team membership decides *which resources* they act on. Combine the two for clean scoping.

4. API and tokens#4-api-and-tokens

API access is configured at Settings → Admin & API, which shows the auto-provisioned IRM API base URLs and an API Tokens section (Create New Token → name + permissions; the token is shown once and can't be retrieved later).

There are two auth methods for the OnCall API:

  • Grafana Cloud service account token (recommended) — granular, rotatable, centrally managed. Send Authorization: Bearer <token> and X-Grafana-URL: https://your-stack.grafana.net.
  • Legacy OnCall API key — user-specific; send Authorization: <key> (no Bearer, no X-Grafana-URL). Only the creating user can see or manage it.

Service-account token limitations to know: they can't retrieve the caller's own user profile, can't perform state-changing alert-group actions (acknowledge/resolve/silence — use a user token or the UI), and /info, /make_call, /send_sms still require a legacy key. A 500 usually means the endpoint doesn't accept service-account auth or the token lacks permissions.

The API is rate-limited to 300 requests per API key per minute; over-limit calls return HTTP 429.

Token best practices

prefer service-account tokens over personal/legacy keys; grant only the permissions each token needs; store tokens in a secrets manager or Terraform variables, never in source control; rotate them on a schedule; and hand out the API Keys Editor role sparingly, since it also confers the ability to use the API.

5. Org-level behavior toggles (Admin & API)#5-org-level-behavior-toggles-admin-api

These toggles change how the whole stack behaves — set them deliberately and document why:

  • Notification channel visibility — show/hide channels (Slack, Teams, …) in users' personal notification rules. On by default when an integration is installed. Hiding a channel only removes it from user profiles; it does not disable the integration itself.
  • Manually manage direct paging integrations (manually_manage_direct_paging_integrations) — turn on to stop IRM auto-creating per-team direct-paging integrations so you can manage them as code. Existing integrations are kept. Required before managing direct_paging integrations in Terraform.
  • Prefer important notification policy — direct pages via Slack use the recipient's *important* personal policy rather than default.
  • Require resolution notes (is_resolution_note_required) — responders must add a resolution note before resolving an alert group. Enforced in the UI, Slack, and bulk resolve — but not by the public API resolve endpoint.
  • Require team membership for updates — restrict write operations to members of the resource's team, across UI, mobile, Slack, Teams, and API. Bypassed by Admin/IRM-Admin, service accounts, and a user notified for a specific alert group (scoped to that one group). Read access is unaffected.
  • Disable schedule updates in the past — enforce future-only editing of web schedules (no past-dated overrides/swaps; rotation edits only via "Save as new"). Doesn't apply to Terraform/API schedules — use the until parameter there instead.

Admins also manage users' linked Slack profiles from IRM → Users (auto-synced when Grafana and Slack emails match).

6. ChatOps configuration#6-chatops-configuration

Chat integrations include Slack, Microsoft Teams, and Telegram. A few placement facts save confusion:

  • Workspace connection is done in the Grafana Cloud portal, not the Settings tab — install the Grafana Cloud app for Slack, then each user links their account under Profile → IRM tab → Slack connection.
  • One Slack workspace can serve multiple stacks, but you can't connect multiple workspaces to a single stack.
  • Org-level Slack behavior lives under IRM → Integrations → Apps → Slack: incident channel auto-creation, invite-on-create, auto-archive (30–60 days recommended, with a 24-hour warning), the channel name template (default {{ prefix }}-{{ date }}-{{ title }}), acknowledge-reminder and auto-unacknowledge timeouts, and rich link previews. These appear only when Slack is installed.

Updating ChatOps settings requires the IRM Admin RBAC role (or the ChatOps Editor role); Editor RBAC explicitly can't change them.

7. Incident-response settings (Incidents tab)#7-incident-response-settings-incidents-tab

Configured at Settings → Incidents — and because incident features are basic-role only, this needs the Admin basic role.

  • Severities — defaults Critical, Major, Minor, Pending; new incidents default to Pending (configurable). Custom severities have a Label and a numeric Level that is immutable after creation; map custom levels to the defaults (P1→Critical, etc.).
  • Statuses — defaults Active and Resolved; add custom statuses with name, icon, and color; reorder by drag.
  • Roles — defaults Commander and Investigator; add custom roles and flag the critical ones as key roles.
  • Custom fields — Text, Number, Single-select, Multi-select; mark a field Mandatory only when it truly must be completed before resolution.
  • Prefixes, announcements, and briefings — channel prefixes on the Incidents tab; announcements and briefings under customize-incident-response.

Keep the incident workflow small and meaningful: a handful of clear severities, statuses that mirror your real process, and mandatory fields reserved for the few that matter.

8. Who can change settings#8-who-can-change-settings

  • Incidents tab — Admin basic role (no RBAC path).
  • Admin & API / org settings — IRM Admin RBAC or Admin basic role; fine-grained equivalents are Settings Editor (other-settings:write), ChatOps Editor, API Keys Editor, and User Settings Admin. Editor RBAC cannot change admin settings, ChatOps, or other users' settings.
  • Team Access Management — admins manage all teams, but must be a member of a team to manage its resources.
  • Assigning any role requires Organization Administrator access.

Reducing Alert Fatigue

A practical guide to cutting alert noise and protecting responders from burnout using Grafana Cloud IRM. Alert fatigue isn't one setting — it's the sum of grouping, routing, escalation, auto-resolution, alert quality, and on-call balance. This guide pulls those levers together. It draws on, and cross-references, the On-Call Schedules, Escalation Chains, Integrations, and Insights guides.

1. Why alert fatigue matters#1-why-alert-fatigue-matters

Alerting on transient failures creates noise, and noise trains responders to ignore alerts — so the one real page hides among ten that didn't matter. The cost is two-fold: missed incidents (the signal is tuned out) and burnout (over-paged, unfairly loaded on-call engineers leave). Grafana's own framing puts the single biggest lever bluntly:

> "Alert grouping is one of the most impactful configurations in IRM. The difference between good and poor grouping can mean receiving 1 phone call instead of 10 at 4am."

The guiding philosophy across Grafana's alerting guidance: favor fewer, high-quality, actionable alerts over many low-value ones. If no action is possible, it shouldn't be an alert — use a dashboard. Alerts behave like technical debt: easy to create, costly to maintain, hard to remove. Reducing fatigue is the ongoing discipline of keeping that debt low.

The levers, roughly in order of impact: group related alerts, fix quality at the source, shape escalation (waits, throttling, sparing use of "Important"), auto-resolve cleanly, balance the roster, and measure and iterate.

2. Grouping — the primary lever#2-grouping-the-primary-lever

Grouping collapses many related alerts into one alert group, so responders get one notification instead of ten. IRM renders a Grouping Id template (Jinja2) against each payload; a payload joins an existing *open* group with the same identifier on the same route, or starts a new one. Two rules to internalize: routing happens before grouping (alerts on different routes never group together, even with identical IDs), and a group is *open* while Firing, Acknowledged, or Silenced — a Resolved group is closed, so a new match starts a new group.

For Grafana Alerting and Alertmanager, keep the default

{{ payload.groupKey }} and configure grouping at the source (notification policies / Alertmanager routing — commonly alertname + grafana_folder, or alertname + namespace + cluster). Changing this template makes IRM apply status updates to the wrong group and breaks auto-resolution.

For other (webhook) sources, compose a stable key:

StrategyGrouping Id template
By alert name{{ payload.commonLabels.alertname }}
By service{{ payload.commonLabels.service_name }}
By service + severity (separates critical from warning){{ payload.commonLabels.service_name }}:{{ payload.commonLabels.severity }}
No grouping (every alert its own group)empty / unique id
Good keys

alertname, service_name, namespace, groupKey. Never group by status fields (Firing/Resolved/OK — splits fire and resolve into different groups and breaks auto-resolve), timestamps, or unique per-event IDs (every alert becomes its own group — the opposite of grouping). Group along operational boundaries (service/owner): a single database failure that fires latency, error-rate, and internal-error alerts should be one group with one root cause, not three pages.

3. Fix alert quality at the source#3-fix-alert-quality-at-the-source

The cheapest noisy alert to silence is the one you never create. Upstream discipline (in Grafana Alerting, addressed fully in a separate engagement, but worth stating here) is the foundation:

  • Alert on symptoms, not infrastructure. Page on user-facing signals (latency, error rate, availability). Infrastructure events (pod restarts, disk) are noisy as pages — route them to dashboards or low-severity channels.
  • Every alert must be actionable. If a firing alert needs no action, it's noise — remove it. Link a runbook and dashboard in annotations so the first responder can act.
  • Mitigate flapping. Set a pending period so a condition must persist before firing; smooth with averages/percentiles over a window instead of raw values; use keep_firing_for or recovery thresholds to prevent rapid resolve-and-refire churn.
  • Scope with multi-dimensional rules (one rule, many label instances) rather than one rule per host; start with a single dimension and add more only when it aids action.
  • Graduate frequent symptom alerts into SLOs — burn-rate alerting reacts to sustained user impact rather than every minor deviation.
  • One integration per team, scoped with source-side label selectors, so routing and grouping stay clean and owned.

4. Shape escalation to space out noise#4-shape-escalation-to-space-out-noise

Even good alerts become fatiguing if escalation fires everything at once. Escalation chains are where you pace and gate notifications:

  • Wait steps space notifications. Documented guidance: 1 minute (urgent), 5 minutes (standard), 15 minutes (lower priority), 30–60 minutes (informational). Start with longer waits and shorten based on measured response time.
  • Volume throttling — the *"Notify if number of alerts in time window"* step (Terraform/API num_alerts_in_window + num_minutes_in_window) continues only if more than X alerts occur in Y minutes (e.g. more than 5 in 30 minutes). This stops sporadic low-priority alerts from paging while still escalating patterns that signal a real problem.
  • Time-based escalation — *"Continue if current UTC time is in range"* routes business-hours vs after-hours differently.
  • Cap repeats — the Repeat escalation step is limited to 5 repeats to prevent infinite loops.
  • Use Important sparingly. Each notify step uses the responder's Default or Important personal rules. Overusing Important erodes its meaning — reserve it for genuine escalation (the pattern is notify default → wait → notify important).
  • Reserve notify-whole-channel for a last resort; it pings every user in the Slack channel.
  • End every chain definitively (resolve, notify-all, or a capped repeat) so escalation can't run — or nag — indefinitely.

Watch three escalation signals when tuning: time to first acknowledgment, escalation depth (steps before response), and false escalations (escalated without needing a human). Frequent false escalations argue for throttling or better alert quality; deep escalations argue for shorter waits or more channels.

5. Auto-resolve cleanly and suppress deliberately#5-auto-resolve-cleanly-and-suppress-deliberately

Resolve signals and suppression, done wrong, become their own noise:

  • Enable Autoresolve (allow_source_based_resolving) for fire-and-resolve sources, with a correct resolve condition template (resolve_condition_template), e.g. {{ payload.status == "resolved" }}. With it off, IRM creates already-resolved alert groups for every resolve signal — clutter. (And never group by status, which breaks this.)
  • Prefer time-limited silences over "Silence forever." A silenced group stays open and keeps absorbing matching alerts without notifying; a "Silence forever" group does so indefinitely. Use time-bounded silences for known/temporary issues and audit any forever-silenced groups periodically.
  • Use maintenance mode for planned work — three modes, always with a set duration:
ModeBehaviorUse for
Silence escalationsAlerts created, escalations pausedTesting integrations/templates
Group alertsEverything into one maintenance group, no escalationPlanned maintenance windows
Disable alertsIncoming alerts ignored entirelyMajor infrastructure changes
  • Heartbeats guard the opposite failure — silence when you *should* be paged. Configure a heartbeat (timeout 1 minute – 24 hours, longer than your monitoring interval) on critical integrations; it auto-resolves when the heartbeat resumes.

6. Balance the human side#6-balance-the-human-side

Fatigue is also about *who* carries the load, not just how much:

  • Watch the schedule balance score ("how evenly work is distributed"), alongside coverage percentage and overloaded users ("team members with significantly more on-call time"). Adjust rotations for anyone consistently overloaded.
  • Enable gap and empty-shift reports (enabled_reports) so coverage holes don't turn into missed pages.
  • Distribute fairly — account for holidays and time off, and use follow-the-sun (user groups per time zone) so nobody is paged through their night.
  • Track per-user notification load with the user_was_notified_of_alert_groups_total metric to spot individuals being over-paged.
  • Prefer shift swaps over overrides for planned changes, set rotation_start explicitly, and enable timezone support.

*(A common industry rule of thumb — not official Grafana guidance — is to avoid more than ~5 consecutive on-call days. Grafana endorses weekly rotations for work-life balance but doesn't publish a specific cap.)*

7. Measure and iterate#7-measure-and-iterate

You can't reduce what you don't measure. IRM exports four metrics (Prometheus format, prefix grafanacloud_oncall_instance_, in the grafanacloud-usage data source):

MetricWhat it tells you about fatigue
alert_groups_total (by integration, state)Which integrations are noisiest, and how many groups sit firing/acknowledged
alert_groups_response_timeMTTA proxy (start → first action), rolling 7 days
alert_groups_resolution_timeMTTR proxy (start → resolved), rolling 7 days
user_was_notified_of_alert_groups_total (by username)Per-user notification load — who is over-paged

Use the pre-built Alert Groups Insights dashboard (tag irm) to rank noisy integrations, and the insight logs to audit config changes. The improvement loop: each month, rank the noisiest integrations by alert_groups_total{state="firing"}, fix grouping and thresholds on the top offenders, add throttling where appropriate, and feed every post-incident review back into thresholds and escalation.

Grafana documents a 95% target for the share of alerts matching specific (non-default) routes rather than the catch-all. Two commonly cited targets — under 5 pages per on-call shift and under a 10% false-positive rate — are useful industry rules of thumb but are not published Grafana numbers; treat them as goals to set with your teams.

8. Low-noise patterns as code#8-low-noise-patterns-as-code

Provisioning consistent, low-noise patterns as code stops fatigue from creeping back in team by team. Terraform-manageable levers (provider grafana/grafana, grafana_oncall_* resources):

  • Integrations — grouping_id_template, allow_source_based_resolving = true, resolve_condition_template on grafana_oncall_integration.
  • Escalation — waits, volume throttling (num_alerts_in_window/num_minutes_in_window), and capped repeats as ordered grafana_oncall_escalation steps.
  • Schedules — rotation_start, use_tz, enabled_reports on grafana_oncall_schedule / shifts.

    escalation_chain_id = grafana_oncall_escalation_chain.payments_warning.id
  }

    grouping_key   = "{{ payload.commonLabels.service_name }}:{{ payload.commonLabels.severity }}"
    resolve_signal = "{{ payload.status == \"resolved\" }}"

  num_minutes_in_window = 30

9. Anti-fatigue checklist#9-anti-fatigue-checklist

  • Keep {{ payload.groupKey }} for Grafana Alerting / Alertmanager; group at the source
  • Group by stable keys (alertname, service_name, namespace) — never status, timestamps, or unique IDs
  • Enable Autoresolve with a correct resolve condition on fire-and-resolve integrations
  • Add wait steps between notifications; start long, shorten by measured response time
  • Add volume throttling to low-priority chains; cap repeats (max 5); end every chain definitively
  • Use Important notifications sparingly; reserve notify-whole-channel for last resort
  • Route the default route to a real chain; target ≥95% specific-route coverage; use labels not regex
  • Prefer time-limited silences; audit "Silence forever" groups; use maintenance mode (with a duration) for planned work
  • Fix alerts at the source: symptom-based, actionable, pending periods, remove non-actionable rules, graduate frequent ones to SLOs
  • Add heartbeats to critical integrations
  • Balance the roster: watch balance score / overloaded users, enable gap reports, prefer swaps, use follow-the-sun
  • Instrument and iterate: watch the four metrics, rank noisy integrations monthly, tune, feed reviews back

Responder Notification Setup

A practical guide to how individual responders configure the way they get paged in Grafana Cloud IRM — personal notification rules, the mobile app, phone/SMS, ChatOps linking — and how admins drive adoption at scale. This is the foundation the rest of IRM depends on: a perfectly designed schedule and escalation chain page nobody if the person on-call never set up how they're reached. Companion to the On-Call Schedules, Escalation Chains, Integrations, Insights, and Incident Management guides.

1. Why this matters most#1-why-this-matters-most

Chains and schedules decide *who and when*. Personal notification rules decide *how* — which of a responder's devices and apps actually ring. If that last step is missing, everything upstream fails silently.

The single most important fact to internalise: email is the default channel for every user. A responder who configures nothing else can only be reached at the email address on their Grafana account — no push, no SMS, no call. This is the number-one onboarding gap, and it fails quietly: the schedule looks healthy, the chain fires, and the page lands in an inbox nobody is watching at 3am.

2. The two chains: Default and Important#2-the-two-chains-default-and-important

Every responder configures notification rules in Profile → IRM tab → Notification rules (also editable from the IRM mobile app's Settings tab). There are two separate, independently configured chains:

  • Default notification rules — used for standard escalation events. Best kept less intrusive (Slack, email, push).
  • Important notification rules — triggered when an escalation step is marked "important." These bypass the Default chain entirely, so they should be the loud, hard-to-miss methods (critical push, phone call, SMS).

An escalation step's Default / Important toggle is what selects which of the responder's two chains fires. A step marked important runs the person's Important rules and skips their Default rules. Because Important is the escape hatch that pierces DND and calls phones, use it sparingly in chain design — overuse erodes the signal (see the Escalation Chains guide).

Both chains must be configured.

A responder with only a Default chain gets nothing extra when a critical alert marks a step important — it simply falls back to whatever Default holds, or to email.

3. Building a chain#3-building-a-chain

In the Notification rules UI, each step is either a Notify by (a delivery method) or a Wait (a timing gap). Add steps, then drag to reorder; they execute top to bottom from position 0.

Available notification methods:

MethodWhen to reach for it
Mobile push (default)Recommended primary channel
Mobile push important / criticalLoud, DND-piercing — for the Important chain
Slack / Microsoft Teams / TelegramFast, low-friction messaging
SMSBackup for critical alerts
Phone callLast-resort, hard-to-ignore
EmailNon-urgent / summary only
Webhook (personal)Custom personal automation
Wait

steps use a fixed set of durations: 1, 5, 15, 30, or 60 minutes (60/300/900/1800/3600 seconds). There is no automatic per-method retry — you build fallback yourself by chaining a notify, then a wait, then a louder notify.

A sensible Default chain (from the docs): Slack immediately → mobile push after 5 minutes → SMS after 15 minutes → phone call after 30 minutes.

A sensible Important chain: critical mobile push immediately → SMS after 1 minute → phone call after 5 minutes.

The principle: layer from least intrusive to most intrusive, with waits between, so you escalate your *own* attention automatically.

4. The IRM mobile app#4-the-irm-mobile-app

The mobile app should be most responders' primary channel — it's the most reliable and the only one that can pierce Do Not Disturb cleanly. Note it has no offline support; it needs connectivity to receive pushes.

Install and pair.

Search "Grafana" in the App Store or Google Play. Sign in either by entering your stack URL (e.g. yourstack.grafana.net) and authenticating with Grafana.com credentials, or by tapping "connect app via QR code" and scanning the QR from your Grafana Cloud profile. Add multiple stacks via the Connected accounts screen if you span environments.

Two push types

, which you add to your notification rules like any other method: *Mobile push default* (follows the app's Default notification settings) and *Mobile push important* (louder/urgent, uses Important settings).

Critical alerts — the step people miss:

  • iOS: enable Critical Alerts at the OS level (Settings → Notifications → Grafana IRM → Critical Alerts), then enable "Override DND for important" in-app. Without the OS toggle, important pushes are just normal pushes.
  • Android: each notification type maps to a system notification channel; set DND override and sound per-channel via "Customize notifications," and toggle "Override system volume" in-app. Prefer Pixel or Samsung devices — some manufacturers (Xiaomi, OnePlus) aggressively suppress notifications. If pushes are unreliable, disable "Pause app activity if unused" and set Battery to Unrestricted.
Sync the IRM contact

(Settings → Sync Grafana IRM contact) to create a phone contact holding all IRM numbers, so calls are recognisable and can be set to bypass DND via iOS Emergency Bypass.

Always send a test notification

(Settings → Push notifications → Send test notification, for both Default and Important) after setup. Untested is unconfigured.

The app also shows your on-call status and upcoming shifts, and lets you create and accept shift swaps on the go, with pushes for swap requests and for shifts starting within 15 minutes.

5. Phone, SMS, and ChatOps linking#5-phone-sms-and-chatops-linking

Phone verification

(Profile → IRM tab → Phone verification): enter your number in E.164 format — +, country code, local number, no spaces/dashes/parentheses, and drop any national trunk 0 (UK 020… becomes +4420…). Get the code by SMS ("Send verification code") or automated voice call ("Call to get the code" — use this for landlines). Then add SMS or Phone call steps to your chains.

Worth teaching responders about live calls: press 1 to acknowledge, 2 to resolve, 3 to silence 30 minutes, 4 to repeat. Voicemail does *not* acknowledge or resolve — only a live keypress does. SMS bundles additional alerts within a 2-minute window into one message; Default and Important alerts bundle separately. Calls and SMS incur no extra cost, but a few regions are unsupported (Crimea, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Syria). Add the IRM source numbers to your contacts so pages are recognisable.

Personal ChatOps linking

(Profile → IRM tab; each requires an admin to have installed the integration first):

  • Slack — Slack connection → authorise your account. Required for auto-updated on-call Slack user groups and @mentions.
  • Microsoft Teams — MS Teams connection → send the linkUser command to the IRM bot.
  • Telegram — Telegram connection → send the verification code to the bot.
Admin fallback

a user who can't self-verify a phone can be marked verified by an admin holding the User Settings Admin RBAC role (IRM → Users → user → Overview → Phone connector → Mark as verified). Paid orgs only.

6. Roles: who can actually be paged#6-roles-who-can-actually-be-paged

Responders need the right role, or they won't receive notifications. Basic roles are Viewer (read-only), Editor, and Admin. For notification purposes the accurate framing is via RBAC:

  • A plain Viewer/Reader is read-only and isn't set up as an active responder.
  • OnCaller is the role "designed for on-call responders" — read access to IRM resources plus the ability to edit alert groups, schedules, shift swaps, and their own user settings.
  • Notifications Receiver — "can receive alert notifications and edit their own IRM user settings" — is the minimal responder grant, optionally paired with User Settings Editor.

Assign OnCaller (or Notifications Receiver) to anyone who goes on-call. Admins also control which channels exist: a channel must be installed under IRM → Integrations *and* enabled under IRM → Settings → Admin & API before it appears in users' personal rules.

7. Driving adoption at scale#7-driving-adoption-at-scale

Because email-only fails silently, adoption is an active job, not an assumption. The recommended sequence (from the notify-responders overview):

  1. Ensure all users have configured notification channels.
  2. Each responder sets up both Default and Important rules.
  3. Build escalation chains that reference them.
  4. Mark critical steps "important."
  5. Test the full flow end to end with a non-production alert.
Spotting gaps.

There is no built-in "users without notification rules" report, so checking is per-user: IRM → Users → a user's Overview tab shows their Phone connector, Mobile App connector, and linked Slack. Admins can see linked Slack profiles across all users from the Users list. The scalable alternative is to provision baseline rules centrally as code (next section) rather than relying on each individual.

Make it part of onboarding.

Bake "configure IRM notification rules, install the mobile app, verify your phone, send a test notification" into the checklist for every new team member — the same day they're added to a schedule.

8. Notification rules as code#8-notification-rules-as-code

Personal notification rules are manageable as code — the scalable way to guarantee every responder has a working baseline instead of hoping they set one up.

Terraform

The grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule resource (requires OnCall provider ≥ v1.8.0) models one step. Look up each user with the grafana_oncall_user data source, then define their chain. The two chains are distinguished purely by important = true/false, and position restarts at 0 within each chain.

terraform
data "grafana_oncall_user" "my_user" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  username = "my_username"
}

# --- Default chain ---
resource "grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule" "default_1" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  user_id  = data.grafana_oncall_user.my_user.id
  position = 0
  type     = "notify_by_mobile_app"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule" "default_2" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  user_id  = data.grafana_oncall_user.my_user.id
  position = 1
  duration = 600 # 10 minutes
  type     = "wait"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule" "default_3" {
  provider = grafana.oncall
  user_id  = data.grafana_oncall_user.my_user.id
  position = 2
  type     = "notify_by_phone_call"
}

# --- Important chain ---
resource "grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule" "important_1" {
  provider  = grafana.oncall
  user_id   = data.grafana_oncall_user.my_user.id
  important = true
  position  = 0
  type      = "notify_by_mobile_app_critical"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule" "important_2" {
  provider  = grafana.oncall
  user_id   = data.grafana_oncall_user.my_user.id
  important = true
  position  = 1
  duration  = 300 # 5 minutes
  type      = "wait"
}

resource "grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule" "important_3" {
  provider  = grafana.oncall
  user_id   = data.grafana_oncall_user.my_user.id
  important = true
  position  = 2
  type      = "notify_by_phone_call"
}

Step type values: notify_by_mobile_app, notify_by_mobile_app_critical, notify_by_sms, notify_by_phone_call, notify_by_slack, notify_by_telegram, notify_by_msteams (Cloud only), notify_by_email, and wait (with duration from the fixed set 60/300/900/1800/3600). Import existing rules with terraform import grafana_oncall_user_notification_rule.name "{{ id }}".

Provision a whole team

by pairing grafana_oncall_user with a for_each over a list of usernames and a module that stamps out the same baseline Default and Important chains for everyone — the reliable answer to "make sure everyone has rules." Responders can still add personal preferences on top; a code-managed baseline just guarantees nobody is email-only.

API

The OnCall HTTP API exposes personal_notification_rules — note it requires user-scoped authentication (a user API token, not an org token) and the user-settings permission:

  • POST {{API_URL}}/api/v1/personal_notification_rules/ — body: user_id (required), type (required), position (optional; -1 appends), duration (for wait), important (default false).
  • GET .../personal_notification_rules/ — filter by user_id, important; returns Default and Important rules together, each with its own position sequence.
  • GET/DELETE .../personal_notification_rules/<ID>/.

As-code best practices

  • Provision a baseline for every responder rather than relying on manual setup — this is the single highest-value use of the resource.
  • Keep it a floor, not a ceiling: let responders refine their own chains on top of the baseline.
  • Version the rules in Git with PR review, alongside the schedules and chains that invoke them.
  • Remember the auth model: notification-rule writes need user-scoped tokens with the user-settings permission — plan your provisioning pipeline's credentials accordingly.

9. Quick reference checklist#9-quick-reference-checklist

For each responder:

  • Assigned the OnCaller (or Notifications Receiver) role
  • Both Default and Important notification rules configured — not email-only
  • Default chain layers non-intrusive → intrusive with Wait steps
  • Important chain uses critical push / phone / SMS
  • Mobile app installed and paired (URL or QR)
  • iOS Critical Alerts enabled / Android per-channel DND + volume set

Responder Playbook

For the people who carry the pager. This guide gets you set up to be reached, then walks the lifecycle of a notification — exactly what to do from the moment you're paged. It covers two coordination paths: Path A, teams that run incidents in Grafana Incident, and Path B, teams that run incidents in a separate platform (ServiceNow, Jira, PagerDuty, or a custom tool). It draws on the Responder Notification Setup, Escalation Chains, and Incident Management guides.

Part 1 — Before your shift: make sure you can be reached#part-1-before-your-shift-make-sure-you-can-be-reached

A perfect schedule and escalation chain page *no one* if your notifications aren't set up — and email is the silent default. Do this once, before you're ever on call (full detail in the Responder Notification Setup guide):

  • Configure both notification chains. In Profile → IRM → Notification rules, set up your Default chain (everyday: push, Slack) and your Important chain (critical: mobile push critical, phone call, SMS). An Important escalation step does nothing for you if your Important chain is empty.
  • Install and pair the mobile app, and turn on critical alerts — iOS: enable Critical Alerts at the OS level, then "Override DND for important"; Android: set per-channel DND and volume. Sync the IRM contact so calls can bypass Do Not Disturb.
  • Verify your phone number (E.164 format) for SMS and voice calls.
  • Link Slack (and Teams/Telegram if your org uses them) in your IRM profile.
  • Send a test notification on both chains — untested is unconfigured.

If you can't do all of that, you're not ready to take the pager. The checklist workbook that ships with this playbook tracks it per responder.

Part 2 — The lifecycle of a notification#part-2-the-lifecycle-of-a-notification

When a page arrives, the first three steps are the same no matter how your team runs incidents. Then the path forks based on scope.

The shared start — every page begins here

  1. Answer the page on whatever channel reached you (push, phone, SMS, or Slack).
  2. Acknowledge immediately. This *stops the escalation chain* and signals you're on it — a new alert joining an acknowledged group won't re-trigger escalation. Acknowledge via: phone press 1, Slack Acknowledge button or /ack, mobile Acknowledge, or web Status → Acknowledge.
  3. Triage the alert group. Open the details: read the grouped alerts, labels, and timeline. If it's noise or a known issue you're already fixing, silence it (time-limited — phone press 3 silences for 30 minutes; the group stays open and re-fires when the silence ends). Otherwise, investigate.

Then decide the scope:

  • Routine and self-contained? Fix it, then resolve the alert group (phone press 2, or the Resolve button) and add a resolution note. You're done.
  • Significant, multi-team, or customer-facing? Escalate to a coordinated incident — follow Path A or Path B below.

If you need help at any point, pull people in: add participants on the alert group (web + Add participants, mobile + Invite with Default or Important priority), or in Slack use the Escalate button or /grafana escalate @user. Check whether someone shows as On-call now before paging them.

Path A — you run incidents in Grafana Incident

When the event needs coordination:

  1. From the alert group details, choose Actions → Declare incident (or /grafana incident new <severity> <title> in Slack). Fill in title, severity, and labels.
  2. Attach the contributing alert group(s) — up to 5 per incident — so the technical context travels with the incident. Labels flow from the alert group to the incident automatically.
  3. Coordinate in the auto-created Slack war room: assume roles (Commander/Investigator), keep the timeline current, and track action items as tasks. Post stakeholder status updates.
  4. Mitigate, then resolve — resolve the incident (with a summary) and the alert group, and add a resolution note.
  5. Retrospect — finalize the timeline, run a blameless post-incident review, and let MTTR/MTTA flow into Insights.

Path B — you run incidents in another platform

Here IRM is your paging / on-call layer and the incident record lives in ServiceNow, Jira, PagerDuty, or a custom tool. After the shared acknowledge/triage:

  1. Hand off to the external tool. Depending on your integration, this is automatic or a chain step:
  • ServiceNow — an IRM alert group auto-creates a ServiceNow incident, and status syncs both ways (e.g. Firing→New, Acknowledged→In Progress, Resolved→Resolved); resolution notes can sync too. Resolve in *either* system and the other follows.
  • Jira — declaring an incident auto-creates a Jira issue and the issue status follows the incident status; tasks link bidirectionally. (Jira Cloud only; note IRM only sends Summary/Description/Project/Issue Type on create.)
  • Outgoing webhook (PagerDuty or custom ITSM) — a "Trigger outgoing webhook" escalation step (or an event trigger) creates/updates a ticket. Status does not sync back automatically unless the external tool calls IRM's API.
  1. Coordinate in your incident platform — that tool owns the incident record, comms, and roles.
  2. Resolve & sync. Close the ticket in the external tool, and confirm the IRM alert group is resolved so paging fully stops. With ServiceNow this is automatic; with Jira or a plain webhook, verify it explicitly.
  3. Retrospect in your incident platform.

The golden rule for Path B: the alert group in IRM must end up Resolved, or IRM will keep paging even after your external incident is closed.

Part 3 — Good habits on call#part-3-good-habits-on-call

  • Acknowledge first, investigate second. Acknowledging is cheap and stops the noise for everyone; you can always resolve or escalate after.
  • Silence deliberately. Use short, time-limited silences for known issues during a fix; avoid "Silence forever."
  • Always leave a resolution note — future-you (and the retrospective) will thank you. Your org may require one.
  • Escalate early if you're stuck; a second responder beats a prolonged outage.
  • Hand off cleanly at end of shift — make sure nothing is left Firing or in an open silence, and brief the next responder on anything in flight.

When you get paged — the one-page checklist#when-you-get-paged-the-one-page-checklist

  • Answer the page (push / phone / SMS / Slack)
  • Acknowledge immediately (phone 1 · Ack · mobile) — this stops escalation
  • Open the alert group; read alerts, labels, timeline
  • Noise or known issue? Silence (time-limited). Otherwise investigate
  • Need help? Add participants / /grafana escalate @user
  • Decide scope:
  • Routine → fix, Resolve (phone 2), add resolution note — done
  • Significant → Path A: Actions → Declare incident, attach group(s), coordinate, resolve, retrospect
  • Significant → Path B: hand off to ServiceNow/Jira/webhook, coordinate there, close ticket, confirm the IRM group is resolved
  • Add a resolution note; confirm nothing is left Firing
